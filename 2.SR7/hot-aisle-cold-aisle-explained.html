Point the servers at each other

The hot aisle / cold aisle layout is one of the oldest and cheapest ways to cut data center cooling energy. Server racks are arranged so their air intakes face each other across one aisle and their exhausts face each other across the next, so cold supply air and hot exhaust air stop mixing.

An illustrated explainer of ENERGY STAR's guide, “Move to a Hot Aisle/Cold Aisle Layout”, part of its 16 More Ways to Cut Energy Waste in the Data Center series.

How it works

Every server breathes the same way: cool air in at the front, warm air out at the back. The layout simply lines racks up in alternating rows so that two rows of fronts share one aisle (the cold aisle) and two rows of backs share the next (the hot aisle).

In most large facilities this is paired with a raised floor. The computer room air conditioner (CRAC) blows chilled air into the space beneath the floor, which pressurizes it. That air escapes upward only where there are perforated tiles, which are placed in the cold aisles, so it arrives right where the servers draw it in. The warm exhaust gathers in the hot aisles, rises, and drifts back along the ceiling to the CRAC's intake.

What goes wrong without it

If every row faces the same direction, the exhaust of one row becomes the intake of the next. Each row inhales air that's already been warmed, and the rows farthest from the air conditioner run hottest. The only way to protect them is to over-cool the entire room. No raised floor? It still works

Smaller server rooms often sit on a concrete slab. You can still get distinct hot and cold aisles by rearranging the racks and reworking the overhead ductwork: supply diffusers drop cold air into the cold aisles, and return registers and ducts sit directly above the hot aisles.

What you save

Keeping supply and exhaust air apart means the cooling system delivers air at the temperature servers actually need, instead of making up for mixing. Better airflow also lets fans run slower and makes it possible to rely more often on “free cooling” from air-side or water-side economizers.

What it can cost

  • DowntimeServers must be off while racks are moved.
  • HVAC tuningThe cooling system needs adjusting for the new airflow.
  • CablingNew or rerouted cables for racks in new positions.
  • Power distributionPower feeds to the racks may need to be reconfigured.

Tips before you start

Get rid of solid rack doors

The layout depends on air passing straight through the rack. Solid glass or Plexiglas doors block it, so remove them or swap them for perforated doors.

Control cooling from the cold aisle

Many CRAC units decide how hard to work based on the temperature of the air coming back to them. Once exhaust is concentrated in hot aisles, that return air is much warmer than before, so the reading becomes misleading. Base control on server inlet temperature in the cold aisle instead.

Retrofits take planning

The layout makes sense for almost any new build or expansion. Rearranging an existing room is harder, since each rack move typically follows this order:

  1. Shut down everything in the rack.
  2. Label and unplug every cable.
  3. Have an electrician realign power so each device has an outlet in its new spot.
  4. Work with facilities to reassess HVAC, because airflow in the room will change substantially.

Watch for new risks

The new arrangement can leave the existing HVAC system ineffective, inefficient or undersized for the new airflow, and it can create fresh hot spots. Plan the cooling changes together with the rack moves rather than afterwards.
