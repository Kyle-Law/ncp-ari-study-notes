Troubleshooting signal integrity

A link that won't come up and a link that's up but corrupting bits are different problems with different causes. Start by working out which one you have, then find the lowest layer that's unhealthy and change one thing at a time.

Exam objectiveTroubleshoot signal integrity issues (dirty connectors, over-bending, BER, link down vs. high BER issues).

In one minute

  • Link down: no light or no lock. Typical causes: unplugged or unseated module, broken fiber, wrong polarity, wrong media/optic type, FEC/speed mismatch, dead lane, far end down.
  • Link up, high BER: signal arrives but degraded. Typical causes: dirty connectors (often with Rx power still "normal"), over-bending or crushed cable, marginal or damaged cable/module, too long a run, receiver overload, missing FEC.
  • BER = errored bits ÷ total bits. PAM4 links rely on FEC: raw (pre-FEC) BER can be high and still be corrected. Effective (post-FEC) errors are what hurt. The IEEE target is about 10⁻¹².
  • Signal ladder: Rx power → signal detect → lock → link up. The highest healthy rung tells you where to look.
  • Isolate: clean first, then swap the cable, swap ends (does the fault follow?), loopback, replace one part at a time, and re-test after each change.

    BER in numbers

    A BER sounds tiny until you multiply it by the line rate. Raw BER on PAM4 links is expected to be well above 10⁻¹². FEC brings the effective rate down.

    Where to read it

    # InfiniBand host / switch
$ mlxlink -d <dev> -c        # raw (pre-FEC) and effective (post-FEC) physical BER, FEC mode
$ perfquery                  # SymbolErrorCounter, LinkErrorRecoveryCounter, LinkDownedCounter
$ ibdiagnet                  # fabric-wide link errors and cable info
# Cumulus / Ethernet
$ ethtool -S swp1 | grep -i err     # uncorrected (post-FEC) errors
$ sudo l1-show swp1 --pcs-errors    # FEC-corrected errors

    Counters also jump during link up/down events. Watch whether they keep climbing over time instead of trusting one snapshot.

    The physical culprits

    Dirty connectors

    The top cause of optical trouble. In Dell's study, most "failed" optics returned had no fault. Rx power can sit in range while errors climb. Inspect and clean before replacing anything.

    Over-bending

    Tight bends leak light out of the core (macrobend loss) and can crack glass. Look for kinks at ports, manager entries and tray drops. A VFL glows at the bend.

    Crush & tension

    Over-tight ties, stacked bundles, pulled jackets. Microbends add loss and errors without visible damage.

    Seating & keying

    A half-latched module or connector gives intermittent errors or flapping. Reseat both ends.

    Wrong part

    Wrong reach class, wrong fiber type, unsupported module, or FEC/speed mismatch between ends.

    Too much light

    A long-reach optic on a short link can overload the receiver and cause errors. Use an attenuator or the right reach.

    Exam traps

    • "Rx power is in range, so it's not the fiber."Dirty end-faces often leave power in range while corrupting data. Clean first.
    • Replacing the transceiver first.Clean, reseat and swap the cable before replacing hardware. Most returned optics test fine.
    • Treating raw BER as failure.PAM4 links run with nonzero raw BER and FEC corrects it. Watch effective/uncorrected errors and the trend.
    • Changing several things at once.You won't know what fixed it. Change one thing, re-test.
    • Link down blamed on dirt alone.Also check polarity, module type, FEC/autoneg and both ends' config.

    Check yourself

    1. A link is up, Rx power is normal on all lanes, but symbol errors keep climbing. First action?

    Contamination can degrade data while leaving power readings acceptable. It's also the cheapest fix to try.

    2. What does FEC change about BER?

    FEC adds redundancy so the receiver repairs errors. Effective BER is what applications see.

    3. Rx power is dark on one lane of a 4-lane optic and the link is down. Likely cause?

    One dark lane kills the whole link. Inspect that MPO position and swap the cable or module to isolate.

    4. The fault moves with the transceiver when you swap ends. What's bad?

    If the fault follows the module, the module is the culprit. If it stays with the port, suspect the port.

    5. At 400 Gb/s and BER 10⁻¹², roughly how often is there an errored bit?

    400 × 10⁹ bits/s × 10⁻¹² = 0.4 errors/s, about one every 2.5 s. That's why FEC exists.
