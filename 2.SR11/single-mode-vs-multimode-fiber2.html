Single-mode vs multimode fiber

Both carry data as pulses of light through glass. The one thing that really separates them is the width of the glass core the light travels in, and almost every other difference follows from that.

Visual summary of “Single Mode vs Multimode Fiber: A Complete Comparison Guide”, LINK-PP Knowledge Center, 16 July 2025. Original article.

How light travels inside each one

In single-mode fiber the core is so narrow that light can only take one route, straight down the middle. It is driven by lasers at 1310 nm or 1550 nm.

In multimode fiber the wider core lets hundreds of routes (“modes”) travel at once, some straight, some bouncing at steep angles. It is driven by cheaper VCSEL lasers or LEDs at 850 nm or 1300 nm.

Every ray moves at the same speed. In the multimode fiber the steep rays travel a longer zig-zag path, so they reach the far end later than the straight one. Press replay to watch them arrive.

Why that matters: modal dispersion

Because multimode rays arrive at slightly different times, each sharp pulse of light gets smeared out. Push the distance or the data rate up and neighbouring pulses start to blur into each other, and the receiver can no longer tell a 1 from a 0. Single-mode fiber has only one path, so this effect is essentially absent. That is why multimode distance is limited to hundreds of metres, while single-mode goes tens of kilometres.

Where the money goes

Multimode cable is cheaper, but the bigger saving is in the transceivers at each end: VCSELs are far simpler to make than the lasers single-mode needs. So for short links multimode usually has the lower installed cost. Beyond roughly 500 m, multimode simply stops working and single-mode is the only option. Single-mode also wins on upgrades: to go faster later you swap the optics and keep the cable.

Matching transceivers to the fiber

Transceiver names encode the fiber they need. SR (short reach) modules are multimode. FR, LR, ER and ZR modules are single-mode, with reach growing along that sequence. A module and cable of different types will not work together.

Quick answers

Can I mix single-mode and multimode in one link?

No. The core sizes don't match, so light is lost at the joint and the transceivers are built for one type only. The link may degrade badly or not work at all.

Do they use the same connectors?

They often look identical (LC and SC are common on both), but the ferrule inside is made to different tolerances for each fiber type. Match the connector to the fiber.

Is multimode easier to install?

Yes. The wide core is more forgiving of dust and slight misalignment, so terminating the ends is faster and needs less precision.

Which is better for future upgrades?

Single-mode. Its bandwidth is limited by the optics rather than the glass, so moving to higher speeds usually means replacing only the transceivers.

Where is the industry heading?

The article argues that single-mode is the long-term strategic choice as speeds climb to 400G, 800G and 1.6T, helped by technologies like BiDi (two directions over one strand) and coherent optics. Multimode keeps evolving too, notably OM5 with short-reach wavelength multiplexing.

Keep in mind the source is published by a transceiver vendor, so its product recommendations are promotional. The technical comparison itself matches standard industry guidance. One simplification worth knowing: modern multimode fiber is “graded-index”, so rays curve gently rather than bouncing sharply off the walls. The zig-zag picture is a teaching model, but the conclusion (different paths, different arrival times) holds.
