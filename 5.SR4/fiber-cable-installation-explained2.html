The core idea: three limits you can't exceed

Fiber cable is actually built to take more pulling force than copper — but only when that force goes through the right parts of the cable. Every fiber cable has a datasheet with three numbers that must never be exceeded during installation. Go past any of them and the glass inside can crack, sometimes invisibly, failing weeks or years later.

Where cable goes

The method depends on the job and the cable type. Outdoors (the "outside plant", or OSP) cable is buried directly in the ground, pulled or blown into conduit and innerduct, or strung aerially between poles. Indoors it runs in raceways, trays above ceilings or under raised floors, hangers, conduit, or special ducts it can be blown through with compressed gas. The techniques are much like those for copper cable — the difference is how unforgiving the glass is to mishandling.

Before the pull

  1. Read the manufacturer's instructions. Fiber cable is often built for a specific job, and the maker may have rules that override the general ones below.
  2. Check the cable is long enough. Coming up short means a splice, and every splice needs its own protection and adds loss.
  3. Walk the route. Know the obstacles and choose the method before anything moves.
  4. Aim for a single pull. Fewer handling stages means fewer chances to overstress the cable.

Pulling tension: pull the Kevlar, not the glass

Inside most fiber cables is a layer of aramid yarn (DuPont's Kevlar). It's there specifically to carry pulling load. Attach your pull to those strength members, and the fibers ride along unstressed. Pull on anything else and the load can end up on the fibers.

Pulling by the outer jacket is only acceptable when the manufacturer says the design allows it, and then only with an approved cable grip — which is usually tied into the strength members anyway.

Connect the pull rope or tape through a swivel pulling eye so the cable can't be twisted as tension builds (more on twist below).

Rules for the pull itself

Never exceed the rated maximum tension. Ask the cable maker and the suppliers of conduit, innerduct and lubricant for tension ratings and lubricant guidance.

Lubricate long pulls — and make sure the lubricant is compatible with the jacket. Long means up to roughly 5 km (3 mi) outdoors, or a few hundred metres inside a building.

Prefer a machine puller with tension control, or a breakaway pulling eye that snaps before the cable limit is reached.

For very long outdoor runs (beyond about 4 km / 2.5 mi), start in the middle and pull toward each end, or put automated pullers at intermediate points so the pull stays continuous.

Indoor shortcut for tight-buffer cable: you can pull by the jacket if you wrap the cable five times around a large spool (about 40 cm / 8 in) used as a mandrel and hold it gently. Friction spreads the load along the jacket instead of concentrating it.

Bend radius: 20× while pulling, 10× once installed

Bend a fiber cable too tightly and you can damage it — a kink can break it outright, especially mid-pull. The usual rule of thumb:

Radius, not diameter. The rule gives a radius. A pulley or sheave's size is usually quoted as a diameter, which must be twice the radius. Mixing these up halves your safety margin.

Always check the datasheet. Some cables — high-fiber-count designs in particular — have their own bend specifications that differ from the rule of thumb.

Why it matters beyond the fiber: cable damaged during installation typically voids the manufacturer's warranty. Warranties commonly exclude improper installation, and datasheets state the installation and in-service bend limits explicitly — so exceeding them leaves you owning the failure.

Don't twist the cable

Twist stresses the fibers, and tension on ropes and cable naturally tries to create it. Three habits prevent it.

Vertical runs in buildings

Drop, don't lift. Whenever possible, feed cable down a riser from the top rather than hauling it up — gravity helps instead of adding tension.

Support it often. A long hanging cable pulls on its own jacket. Support it at frequent intervals with cable ties (snug, never deforming the jacket) or Kellems-style mesh grips.

Leave service loops. A loop of spare cable gives you something to grip for support and slack for future repairs or re-routing.

Cable ties: snug is fine, tight is damage

Fiber is sensitive to crushing. A plastic tie cinched with an installation tool can squeeze the cable enough to raise loss (attenuation) or even break fibers. If you use ties, tighten them by hand only until they're snug but can still be slid along the cable, then cut the tail so nobody tightens them later. Better still, use hook-and-loop (Velcro-type) straps, which physically can't apply a damaging crush load.
