Where fiber cable gets installed

Fiber goes in much the same places copper does. Outside, it can be buried directly in the ground, pulled or blown into conduit and innerduct, or strung aerially between poles. Inside buildings it rides in raceways, in cable trays above ceilings or below raised floors, in hangers, in conduit, or is blown through special ducts with compressed gas.

The method depends on the job and the cable type. The key difference from copper: fiber cable can actually take more pulling force than copper — but only when pulled the right way. Stress in the wrong place, or a kink, can damage fibers in ways that fail later.

Before you pull

  1. Read the manufacturer's instructions. Fiber cable is often built for a specific job, and its maker may have installation rules unique to that design.
  2. Confirm the cable is long enough. A short cable means an unplanned splice — which then needs its own protection.
  3. Walk the route. Decide installation methods and spot obstacles before anything moves.
  4. Plan to do it in one pull. Each extra pull is another chance to overstress the cable.
The non-negotiable rule. Every fiber cable has limits for pulling tension, minimum bend radius and crush load. Exceed any one during installation and the damage can be permanent. Installers must know these numbers for the cable in hand.

Pulling tension: pull by the strength members

Inside the jacket, manufacturers include strength members — usually aramid yarn (Kevlar) — specifically for pulling. Attach to those, not to the jacket, unless the cable is designed to be pulled by its jacket and you use an approved grip (which is usually tied to the strength members anyway).

Connect the rope or tape through a swivel pulling eye so the cable can't twist during the pull. Where possible, use an automated puller with tension control and/or a breakaway eye that parts before the cable's rating is reached.

Tight-buffer cable indoors

For premises work, tight-buffer cable may be pulled by the jacket if you use a large spool (about 40 cm / 8 in) as a mandrel: wrap the cable around it five times and hold gently while pulling. The friction spreads the load instead of concentrating it.

Long runs and lubricant

In conduit or innerduct, outside-plant pulls can reach roughly 5 km (3 miles), and premises pulls hundreds of meters — with a lubricant that's compatible with the jacket. Past about 4 km (2.5 miles), pull from the middle toward both ends, or place assist pullers along the route for one continuous pull. Ask the cable, duct and lubricant suppliers for tension and lubricant guidance.

Bend radius: try it

Bend fiber too tightly and it can break or lose light. The general rule: 20× the cable diameter while under pulling tension, and 10× the diameter long-term after installation. Some cables — high fiber count designs in particular — have their own figures, so always check the spec sheet. Bending beyond spec can also void the manufacturer's warranty, since damage from improper installation is typically excluded.

Radius, not diameter. A 13 mm (0.5 in) cable needs a 260 mm (10 in) radius while pulling — so a pulley it runs over must be at least 520 mm (20 in) in diameter. Mixing the two up halves your safety margin.

Don't twist the cable

Twist stresses the fibers, and pulling tension on a rope naturally tries to twist whatever is attached. That's what the swivel pulling eye is for.

Twist also sneaks in at the reel. Roll the cable off the spool by letting the reel turn on an axle. Pulling it off over the end of a stationary reel adds one full twist for every turn of cable.

Why the figure-8?

When you have to lay out loops of cable on the ground — at a midpoint of a long pull, for instance — plain coils build up twist. A figure-8 puts a half twist into the cable on one lobe and takes it back out on the other, so the cable comes off the pile without any.

Vertical runs in buildings

Whenever you can, drop cable down a riser instead of hauling it up — gravity does the work and the pulling end doesn't carry the entire hanging weight.

Support vertical cable frequently so the jacket isn't carrying its own weight over long spans. Snug cable ties (not deforming the jacket) or Kellems-style mesh grips both work. Leave service loops: they give you something to grip for support and spare length for future repairs or rerouting.

Cable ties and crush

Fiber cable is sensitive to squeezing. Ordinary cable ties — especially cinched with a tensioning tool — can deform the jacket enough to add attenuation or even break fibers.

If you use ties, tighten them by hand only until snug but still loose enough to slide along the cable, then cut off the tail so nobody tightens them later. Better still, use hook-and-loop (Velcro-style) straps, which can't apply a damaging crush load.
