Alternatingphasepower

Why data-centre racks run on three-phase power, why the load has to be shared evenly between the phases, and how a PDU that alternates phase on every outlet makes that sharing almost automatic.

An illustrated walkthrough of the Server Technology white paper “Alternating Phase: A New Look at 3-Phase Power Distribution” (July 2019).

Three supplies, one third of a cycle apart

A three-phase feed is three AC voltages on separate wires. The paper calls them X, Y and Z (you’ll also see L1, L2, L3). Each is a normal sine wave, but they peak one after another, 120° apart. Data centres use three-phase because it carries far more power per cable than single-phase, which keeps high-density racks feasible and cable costs down.

The useful trick: when the three phases carry equal current, their waves add up to zero at every instant. Nothing needs to flow back on the neutral, and every conductor does an equal share of the work. When they are unequal, the leftover current has to go somewhere. Drag the sliders to see it.

What an unbalanced rack costs you

The paper points out that installers usually just want the equipment powered on, so balance gets overlooked. It lists three consequences.

Extra heat

Current that doesn’t cancel still flows through wires and connections, and turns into heat. That heat has to be removed, so cooling costs rise.

Higher bills

An unbalanced system is a less efficient one. Losses in the distribution path go up, and you pay for energy that never reaches a server.

Tripped breakers

If one phase carries most of the load, it hits its breaker limit first, either on the PDU or upstream, and the rack loses power even though the other phases had room to spare.

Why the traditional PDU makes balance hard

A conventional three-phase rack PDU groups its outlets into branches: a run of outlets on X, then a run on Y, then a run on Z. Balancing means spreading equipment across those runs. That’s easy if the rack holds identical servers, but real racks mix switches, storage, blade chassis and 1U/2U/3U servers of different brands, each drawing different current.

To hit the right branch, cords get stretched along the length of the PDU. The result is a tangle of cables at the back of the rack that blocks airflow and adds to the heat problem above.

Higher density makes it worse

Denser racks need more kilowatts. At 60 A and 100 A feeds, PDUs split into six, nine or twelve branches, and the load still has to be spread evenly across every one of them.

Try it: fill a rack

The paper’s fix is a PDU that alternates phase on every outlet instead of every branch. Here’s a 42U rack with a 24-outlet PDU (three 16 A branches). Add equipment; it stacks from the bottom up. How the outlets get their phase

Alternating every outlet sounds like a wiring nightmare inside the PDU. Server Technology’s approach (patent pending at the time of the paper) skips internal wires entirely. A multi-layer circuit board carries each phase on its own copper layer, and each outlet’s contact reaches down to one of those layers.

The paper credits this with three benefits: less heat build-up inside the PDU, fewer human wiring errors during manufacturing, and a cleaner design that shrinks the product.

Two more practical wins

Moving a device to a different phase only means moving its plug a few centimetres to the next outlet, so shorter cords can be used and the back of the rack stays tidy.

It also suits basic PDUs with no current display. Without a meter, an installer can’t see which branch is filling up. With alternating outlets there is no long single-phase run to overload, so plugging in from bottom to top carries much less risk of tripping a breaker.

In short

Traditional three-phase rack distribution divides power into separate branches, which makes both load balancing and cabling difficult. Alternating phase on each receptacle gives simpler cabling, better airflow, easier balancing and better efficiency.
