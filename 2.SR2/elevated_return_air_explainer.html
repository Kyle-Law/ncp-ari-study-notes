Run the data hall warmer, spend less on cooling

Why warmer return air helps

A precision air conditioner is a heat pump: it collects heat from the room and rejects it outdoors. How much heat one unit can move depends on two things — how much air passes through it and the temperature difference (ΔT) between the air coming back to it and the air it sends out.

Cooling capacity ∝ airflow × ΔT

If hot exhaust from the servers reaches the coil without being diluted by cold air, the coil sees warmer air, exchanges heat more effectively, and delivers more capacity for the same compressor and fan power. The paper states this holds for virtually all air-conditioning equipment, with some units capped at a maximum return temperature.

The only temperature that really matters is the one at the server intake. The paper notes that current practice lets many rooms run the cold aisle at about 24 °C. Keeping the cold aisle there while the hot aisle runs hot is what the hot aisle / cold aisle layout (drawn above) is for.

Warmer water and air also stretch the hours in which free cooling works — using cool outside air, or outdoor-cooled water, instead of running compressors.

What ASHRAE allows

ASHRAE TC 9.9 publishes the temperature envelopes IT equipment is designed for. The old recommended band was 20–25 °C. It widened to 18–27 °C, and in 2011 ASHRAE added equipment classes whose allowable ranges go up to 45 °C. Classes A3 (40 °C) and A4 (45 °C) exist specifically to enable economisation.

Measuring whether the airflow is working

Raising temperatures only pays off if cold and hot air stay separated. The paper leans on three indices to check that, and one to check overall efficiency.

Return Temperature Index (RTI)

Compares the temperature rise across the cooling unit with the rise across the racks.

RTI = (Treturn − Tsupply) ÷ (Track out − Track in) × 100%

At 100% every bit of cold air passes through a server exactly once. Below 100%, cold air is bypassing the racks and diluting the return. Above 100%, hot exhaust is recirculating into server intakes. Real rooms always have some of each; the goal is to minimise both.

Supply and Return Heat Index (SHI, RHI)

Both are dimensionless. With Q the heat the racks produce and δQ the heat cold air picks up before it enters a rack (from mixing with hot air):

SHI = δQ ÷ (Q + δQ)    RHI = Q ÷ (Q + δQ)    SHI + RHI = 1

A higher RHI means less mixing. The paper treats RHI above 0.8 as acceptable. (Its formulas are images the text extraction dropped; these are the standard definitions.)

Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE)

PUE = total facility power ÷ IT equipment power

Cooling uses 35–40% of a data centre’s power, according to the paper, so it is the largest “grey space” load to attack. The chart follows the slider at the top of the page: it applies the 4–5% per °C figure to cooling energy in an example facility where cooling starts at 38% of the total.

Three common objections

The paper walks through the arguments operators raise against running warmer.

Keeping hot and cold aisles

Newer, denser servers produce a bigger temperature jump from intake to exhaust. With a hot aisle / cold aisle layout, that means hotter return air at the cooling unit, which raises its capacity at the same compressor power and improves PUE.

Verdict: works in favour of higher return temperatures.

Reliability and warranties

Raising temperatures is safe only alongside airflow hygiene:

  • blanking panels in empty rack slots
  • sealed cable cut-outs
  • no cable clutter under the raised floor
  • some form of aisle containment

The paper cites facilities moving from 17 °C to 21 °C and from 21 °C to 24 °C without losing availability or warranty cover.

Verdict: manageable if airflow is fixed first.

Uncomfortable for people

With cold aisle containment the uncontained room can reach 27 °C, and above 38 °C with high-density IT. Lights-out and supercomputing sites with few visitors suit high setpoints best; elsewhere staff need to learn that these temperatures are normal, not a failure.

Verdict: a cultural change more than a technical one.

Can the cooling hardware take it?

Chilled-water (CW) units have no real ceiling on return temperature, and warmer air lets the whole water loop run warmer; the paper cites CW selections at 36–37 °C return air with 20–28 °C water.

Direct-expansion (DX) units are the problem. Their scroll compressors need cool suction gas to cool the motor, and above roughly 28 °C return the gas arrives too hot and can damage the motor. Inverter-driven compressors widen the evaporating envelope and strengthen the motor, which is what makes the paper’s “33–37 °C is now common” claim possible for DX.

Control the supply, let the return float

Most units regulate on return air temperature: they hold the room constant and let supply air wander. As IT load swings, server intakes get whatever comes out. Controlling on the supply side instead makes server-inlet temperature predictable, which is what gives operators the confidence to raise air and water temperatures further. The paper pairs this with intelligent controllers that vary capacity and hold floor pressure constant.

Takeaways

Fix airflow first: containment, blanking, sealing. Measure it with RTI and RHI rather than trusting design specs, since CFD models can miss real obstructions. Then raise the setpoint in steps, keeping server intakes inside ASHRAE’s recommended band. Pick hardware that can handle it (chilled water or inverter DX) and control on supply air. The reward the paper promises is lower energy bills, a smaller carbon footprint and more capacity from the units you already own.

Reading notes

  • This is a vendor application note, so the architecture table points to Vertiv products. The physics and ASHRAE figures are general.
  • The abstract mixes up editions and ranges: it compares a “2008” and “2015” edition, and the 5–32 °C / 8–80% figures it quotes are A1 allowable limits, not the recommended range.
  • The “4–5% per °C” figure is used for both energy cost savings and capacity gain. Treat it as a rule of thumb near typical setpoints, not something that compounds indefinitely.
  • ASHRAE is the American Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air-Conditioning Engineers; the paper expands it slightly differently.
  • The note dates from 2020. ASHRAE has issued newer thermal guidelines since, including a high-density class (H1) with a tighter recommended range, so check the current edition before setting targets.
