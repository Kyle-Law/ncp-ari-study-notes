Why an OTDR test fails, and how to read the trace to find out

A visual walkthrough of Fluke Networks' knowledge-base article on diagnosing OTDR test failures with the DTX Compact OTDR module. It covers what each failed limit usually means and how the common fault shapes look on the trace.

Source: Fluke Networks Knowledge Base (2014). All traces below are redrawn illustrations, not the original figures.

First, how an OTDR sees a fibre

An OTDR (optical time-domain reflectometer) fires short light pulses into one end of the fibre and listens to what comes back. Two things return: a faint, continuous glow scattered back by the glass itself (backscatter), and sharp flashes of reflection wherever light hits an air gap or glass surface, such as a connector or the fibre end. Plotting returned power against distance gives the trace.

A healthy link. Height is returned power (dB); left to right is distance down the fibre.

Downward slope

Normal fibre attenuation. A steady straight slope means uniform glass.

Spike plus a step down

A reflective event: a connector or mechanical splice. The spike is reflection; the step is loss.

Step down, no spike

A non-reflective event: a fusion splice or a bend. Only loss, no reflection.

Big spike, then noise

The fibre end. After it only instrument noise remains.

To measure the first and last connectors of the link properly, testers put a launch fibre in front and a receive (tail) fibre after the link. With launch compensation enabled, the tester subtracts those reference cords from the results.

Overall loss fails

Total loss end to end is over the limit.

  • Dirty or damaged connections: look for high-loss reflective events.
  • Wrong fibre type on the OTDR tab in Setup.
  • A cord, launch fibre or segment of the wrong fibre type. If your cords are right, look for a mismatched section in the link.
  • Bad splice or sharp bend: locate it on the trace or event table.

Largest event fails

One single event has more loss than allowed.

  • It is a dirty or damaged connector.
  • It is a junction between mismatched fibre types.
  • It is a bad splice or a sharp bend.

Length or distance is wrong

A known length reads too long or too short, or an event sits in the wrong place.

  • The fibre is broken, or a connection is open where the trace ends.
  • Wrong fibre type on the Cable tab in Setup.
  • Index of refraction (n) needs adjusting on the Cable tab.
  • Wrong test limit selected on the OTDR tab.

An event fails but is within limits

The event itself is fine; it inherits the fail.

  • The fibre segment before the event exceeded a limit. Press F3 View Details from the Event Table or OTDR Results screen to see the segment result.
Warning instead of fail? Overall loss can show a WARNING if the first or last event is hidden (buried in another event's dead zone). With launch compensation on, the warning appears if the launch event is hidden, or the event right after the receive event is hidden.

Spotting ghosts

A ghost is a fake echo. A strongly reflective connector bounces light back to the OTDR, which bounces it forward again, so the tester sees a second copy of a real reflection at a multiple of the real distance. Ghosts are not faults in themselves, but mid-cable ghosts point to a dirty, highly reflective, badly seated or wrongly polished connector.

The tell-tale signs: ghosts show almost no loss, they fall at whole multiples of the distance to a real connector, and repeats from one source are evenly spaced. On a trace with several ghosts, the event table may name only the first one. Ghosts caused by hidden events may not be labelled as ghosts at all, and ghosts after the fibre end are not listed in the event table.

Why the index of refraction changes length

The OTDR never measures distance directly. It times the round trip of the light and converts time to distance using the speed of light in glass, which depends on the index of refraction n.

distance = (speed of light × round-trip time) ÷ (2 × n)

If the n set on the tester does not match the fibre, every distance on the trace scales by the ratio. Try it:
