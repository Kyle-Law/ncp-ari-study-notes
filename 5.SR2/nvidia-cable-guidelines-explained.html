Explains NVIDIA's Cable Installation and Management Guidelines (last updated May 2023). Diagrams are simplified illustrations, not NVIDIA's figures.

Treat every cable like a thin glass pipe

NVIDIA's networking docs collect lessons from cables returned as damaged. Most of that damage can't be repaired, and almost all of it came from handling: bending, pulling, stepping, crushing, dirt. This page walks through the guidance with a diagram for each idea.

Unpacking and handling

Active cables and transceivers contain electronics, so they're built with enough static (ESD) tolerance for normal data center use, but you should still follow standard ESD precautions when unpacking and plugging them in. Open the packaging in a space that matches the storage humidity listed on the product datasheet; each datasheet also has the specific ESD figures for that part.

The 15 don'ts

These come straight from damage NVIDIA has seen on returned cables. Each card shows the mistake and the safer habit to use instead.

Bend radius: the most common silent killer

Every cable has a minimum bend radius on its datasheet. Bend tighter than that and fiber can crack or leak light, while copper pairs can deform and lose signal quality. Damage isn't always visible, and a kinked cable is effectively scrap. Keep turns as wide as the space allows.

Illustrative only. The page says to follow each product's recommended radius. The 10× figure used here is a common industry rule of thumb for static installs, not an NVIDIA number; always check the datasheet.

Support, hanging and fasteners

A cable's own weight becomes strain on its connector. The guidance: never let a cable hang freely for more than 2 m (7 ft), support it at least every 2 m or lay it in a tray, and bundle with soft hook-and-loop (velcro) ties spaced 30 to 60 cm (12 to 24 in) apart. Hard zip ties and staples crush the jacket and the fibers inside.

Also avoid over-bundling or stacking bundles on top of each other. Cables on the bottom of a heavy pile get compressed and can lose performance.

A suitable working environment

Dust is the main enemy of fiber. Airborne particles are roughly the same size as a single-mode fiber's core, so one speck can block a meaningful share of the light path and scratch the polished connector face. Dirt is the most common cause of scratched connectors and high loss readings.

Keep it clean and cool

Work in a clean area, away from heating vents that blow dust onto connectors. Leave dust caps on every connector, splice, patch panel and unused port until the moment you connect. Keep cables out of direct sunlight and away from condensation, and remove unused cables that restrict airflow and cause overheating.

Keep copper away from noise

Copper cables pick up electromagnetic interference. Route them away from power converters, air conditioners, power cords, fluorescent lighting, building electrical runs and fire-suppression components.

Installing inside the rack

The goal is a rack where every transceiver, fan and power unit can still be pulled out, air still flows, and no connector carries the weight of its cable. That means planning space before you plug anything in: the rack must be wide enough for cables to run between the switch and the side walls, and the cable management system and rack floor should carry the cable weight.

Install one, test one

Test each cable as you install it: connect both ends and confirm a physical and a logical link before moving to the next cable. Finding a bad cable now is far cheaper than hunting for it in a finished bundle.

1. Mountfix the cable to the rack with velcro
2. Pre-bendshape the tip toward its route
3. Connectboth ends, holding the connector
4. Verifyphysical link light and logical link up
5. Nextonly then move to the next cable

Other rack rules: keep cables away from the front of other equipment, don't block airflow or transceiver removal, and avoid routing through pipes and holes that will be full the next time you need to add a run. Plan for switches (such as leaf switches) to be extracted without unplugging their neighbors.

Managing cable runs

Stay under 90% of the rated reach

Use cables of the right length with only a little slack at each end, and keep every run below 90% of the maximum distance its media type supports under the relevant standard.

Group, color and label

Bundle cables by purpose, for example inter-switch links in one group and uplinks to core devices in another. Use colored ties where the color encodes the endpoints, and label every cable at both ends and along the run. Keep copper and fiber in separate runs, never mix 50 µm and 62.5 µm multi-mode fiber in one link, pre-install spare cables for future replacements, and put the main distribution area in the middle of the data center to keep runs short.

Inserting a cable

Grip the connector body near its lower part between your thumb and index finger, hold it square to the port panel, and push gently into the cage. Push on the connector, never on the cable, and never use the pull-tab to insert; the tab is only for removal.

Extracting a cable

For a transceiver or cable with an integrated pull-tab: take the tab, pull firmly but gently straight out, perpendicular to the panel, until the module releases from the cage, then slide it out. Never pull by the cable jacket.
