380 Vdc architectures for the modern data center

A visual walkthrough of the EMerge Alliance white paper (2013). Its argument in one sentence: servers already turn everything into roughly 380 V dc internally, so distribute 380 V dc from the UPS onward and delete the conversion stages in between.

Why the paper was written

The paper starts from the fact that most electricity now ends up in electronics, which run on dc. Every ac→dc and dc→ac stage between the utility and the chip wastes energy and adds parts that can fail. Renewable sources and batteries are also natively dc, so an ac building needs extra converters to connect them.

Data centers have traditionally protected the critical bus at four scales, often combined: geographic redundancy across sites, a centralized redundant UPS per facility, UPS units placed in the equipment row (often as redundant “pods”), and dedicated protection per rack. Each approach spawned its own ac-UPS products and topologies, which the paper calls hard for operators to compare.

The authors also point out a trade-off in ac practice: to gain efficiency, operators sometimes run ac-UPSs in “eco mode”, sending the load through the bypass on raw utility power. That buys efficiency by giving up power quality. The paper offers 380 V dc as a way to improve efficiency without that sacrifice. On naming, 380 V is the normal float voltage with lead-acid batteries and 400 V is the upper limit of normal operation, so the paper treats “380 Vdc” and “400 Vdc” as the same thing. IT equipment is expected to accept 260–400 V dc.

ac-UPS vs dc-UPS: the structural difference

Two differences drive everything else. In an ac-UPS the battery sits in the middle, between rectifier and inverter. In a dc-UPS the battery sits directly on the critical output bus. And a dc-UPS has no bypass at all, because there is no ac utility waveform it could switch the dc load onto. Its redundancy comes from spare rectifier modules instead.

Modular vs monolithic rectifiers

The paper distinguishes a modular rectifier, a plug-in unit one technician can replace without shutting anything down, from a monolithic rectifier, a large fixed-rating converter that needs partial or full shutdown to service. Its example system fills a main cabinet in 15 kW modules up to 120 kW, with extension cabinets and an optional battery cabinet alongside; each module weighs about 10 kg.

Both ac and dc UPSs come in either style, but the paper argues modularity is easier in dc: fewer plug-in interfaces, smaller and lighter units, no synchronisation to parallel them, and fewer components. The designs are borrowed directly from decades of −48 V dc telecom plant.

Mapping onto BICSI 002-2011 availability classes

ANSI/BICSI 002-2011 asks designers to pick a topology class from two criteria: how much annual maintenance downtime is tolerable, and how badly an outage hurts operations. The paper shows that each class has a 380 V dc equivalent that looks like the ac version minus the bypass feeders, which simplifies the upstream switchgear. The paper’s conclusion for this section: one dc-UPS building block covers every class. Use one system for F1 and F2 and two (2N) for F3 and F4, and grow capacity and redundancy by adding modules and batteries. That avoids buying a bypass and switchgear sized for the ultimate load on day one, which leaves “stranded” capacity. Battery recharge time is also tuned by adding rectifiers, rather than oversizing a monolithic UPS.

Alternative dc topologies

Beyond one-to-one equivalents, the paper describes layouts that give up strict BICSI compliance in exchange for lower cost, selective redundancy and less stranded capacity, while still offering enough resilience for many sites.

Practical build-out

The paper stresses that 380 V dc can be wired exactly like ac: discrete wire in conduit, cable on wireways, or busway, subject to the local authority having jurisdiction. In the US, conductors are sized from the same NEC tables. dc-rated breakers and fuses are available off the shelf.

The IT case: looking outward from the chip

CPUs, memory, drives and fans all run on dc between about 1.35 V and 12 V. An ac server power supply first rectifies and boosts to about 380 V dc, then steps down to 12 V. Picking 380 V as the distribution voltage means a dc-input supply is essentially an ac supply with its front end removed.

Three-phase ac also brings a chore dc does not: phase balancing. Single-phase servers must be spread evenly across three phases, and because server draw shifts with configuration and workload, a balanced room today can be unbalanced tomorrow. With dc there are no phases, no power factor and no phase angle. The paper adds that dc conductors avoid skin- and proximity-effect losses and the derating and filtering that harmonics require.

Reliability, cost, safety, efficiency

The paper’s central claim is that availability and total cost of ownership share one driver: simplicity. dc has fewer series-connected parts (breakers, terminals, joints) and no load balancing, so there is less to fail and less room for operator error. The design principles it borrows from telecom:

N+m conversionSeveral rectifiers can fail with no immediate action needed; spares swap in on site.
Distributed controlIf the main system controller fails, power delivery continues.
Long repair windowsRedundancy buys time, so failed parts can wait for scheduled replacement.
Fewer series breakersFewer devices in the path to trip, wear or be misoperated.
No synchronisationParalleling dc sources needs only simple load sharing, not phase matching.
Hot-swap maintenanceModules are serviced live by trained on-site technicians.

The paper says reliability studies indicate that 380 V dc, like 48 V dc, achieves availability several orders of magnitude higher than equivalent ac-UPS systems. It defers detailed treatment of TCO, safety and efficiency to later papers. On safety it says only that the topologies assume full code and listing compliance and that dc systems lend themselves to fast control and response during faults. On efficiency it concedes that ac systems have improved a lot, and positions dc’s advantage around renewables and dc microgrids.

The two standards behind it

ANSI/BICSI 002-2011 is primarily an ac design standard, but it recognises dc power for critical loads in two forms: as the primary source for carrier equipment, and in computer rooms for efficiency, simpler design and easier paralleling of alternative sources. It requires dc systems to meet the same availability targets as ac, with extra attention to personnel safety. The paper argues its topologies meet that objective.

EMerge Alliance Data/Telecom Center Standard v1.0 defines a nominal 380 V dc distribution system between the facility power infrastructure and the equipment in a data or telecom space. Its aims are faster installation, easier reconfiguration, safe power levels under the 2011 NEC, modular building blocks with competitive first cost, lower energy loss, and easy integration of solar, wind, fuel cells and batteries. The first release notes that circuit protection, control and arc-flash guidance will need periodic updates, and the Alliance runs a registry of compliant products. It places the ac→dc conversion at the distribution level rather than in each rack or server.

Reading it critically

This is an advocacy paper from an industry alliance, written with lead contributors from dc equipment vendors. The architectures are explained carefully, but several claims are asserted rather than demonstrated in the paper itself:

  • The “several orders higher availability” claim cites studies without presenting their numbers or assumptions.
  • The busway comparison counts two dc buses (304 kW) against one ac circuit. Per bus, 380 V dc at 400 A is 152 kW, roughly the same as 415 V ac.
  • dc current has no zero crossing, so interrupting faults and managing arc flash is harder than with ac. The paper acknowledges this only indirectly, through the standard’s note that arc-flash guidance needs updating.
  • Economics, efficiency and safety are explicitly left to later papers, so this one does not quantify savings.

For context, as of my most recent information, hyperscale rack designs have largely standardised on 48 V dc inside the rack, while high-voltage dc facility distribution has seen renewed interest for very dense AI racks. Check current sources if you are evaluating this for a real project.

Glossary

N, N+1, N+m
N units carry the load; +1 or +m are spares that let units fail or be removed without dropping the load.
2N, 2(N+1)
Two complete, independent systems, each able to carry the full load (and each with its own spare in 2(N+1)).
Critical bus
The protected output that feeds IT equipment.
Static bypass
An automatic electronic switch in an ac-UPS that transfers the load to raw utility power.
Maintenance bypass
A manual path around the whole UPS so it can be serviced.
Eco mode
Running an ac-UPS with the load normally on the bypass to save energy.
Stranded capacity
Installed capacity that cannot be used, e.g. switchgear bought for the final load but idle for years.
ATS
Automatic transfer switch between utility and generator.
TVSS
Transient voltage surge suppression.
PDU
Power distribution unit feeding individual racks.
AHJ
Authority having jurisdiction, the local body that approves electrical installations.
PSU
The power supply inside a server.
ICT
Information and communications technology equipment.
