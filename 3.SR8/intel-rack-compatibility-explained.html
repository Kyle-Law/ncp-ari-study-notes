Will this server fit this rack?

A visual walkthrough of Intel's Server Rack Cabinet Compatibility Guide, revision 2.4 (September 2005).

The guide is an 18-page checklist for anyone buying a rack cabinet to hold Intel server chassis. It boils down to four questions: is the rack built to the 19-inch EIA-310-D standard, is there room at the mounting posts for Intel's slide rails, is the cabinet deep enough for the server plus its cables, and can enough air flow through it front to back.

The vocabulary

Rack equipment is measured in rack units (U). One U is 44.45 mm (1.75 in) of vertical space, so a "2U server" is 88.9 mm tall. The guide works in millimetres first, inches second, and assumes a standard 19-inch rack throughout.

Rack
An open frame for mounting equipment. Comes in 2-post and 4-post forms.
Cabinet
A free-standing 4-post enclosure, usually with doors and side panels.
Chassis
The metal box that holds a server's electronics.
Bezel
The cosmetic plastic or metal cover on the front of a server.
Mounting flange
The vertical strips with holes, front and rear, that servers and rails bolt to. The front one is the "EIA-310-D mounting flange".
Slide rails
Telescoping rails that let a server slide out of the rack like a drawer while staying supported.
PCI card bulkhead
The rear wall inside a server where expansion-card brackets attach. The guide measures server length to this point (see the appendix).
CFM
Cubic feet per minute, a measure of airflow volume.
IHV
Independent hardware vendor; here, the companies that make rack cabinets.

Two kinds of rack

Information

The guide distinguishes enclosed 4-post cabinets, typical of data centres, from open 2-post racks, which it describes as common in US telecom rooms. Everything that follows about depth, doors and airflow applies to 4-post cabinets.

5.1Built to EIA-310-D

Required

EIA-310-D is the 1992 Electronic Industries Association standard for 19-inch cabinets, racks and panels. It fixes the spacing, mounting and bezel clearances that let any compliant server bolt into any compliant rack. Intel designs its chassis to it, and warns that not every cabinet vendor builds to it, so you should ask. A non-compliant rack may not accept Intel's rail kits at all.

The standard allows three kinds of mounting hole. Intel's rail hardware handles two of them.

5.1.3Room for the rail at the flange

Required

This is the check the EIA standard does not cover, and the one the guide calls absolutely critical. Intel's slide rails need at least 16.77 mm of clearance at the front mounting flange to attach properly.

The shape of the flange decides whether you get it. Many cabinets ship with U-shaped flanges whose offset distance is often under the limit, 15 mm being typical. L-shaped flanges typically leave about 22 mm. If in doubt, the guide's advice is simple: order the cabinet with L-shaped flanges.

5.2Cabinet depth and front clearance

Recommended depth   Required 51 mm front clearance

A cabinet has to hold more than the server. The guide splits the depth into pieces, viewed from above:

D is the gap between the front door and the front mounting flange. It must be at least 51 mm (2 in), because the bezel and pull handles stick out in front of the flange and air has to get in. E is the server's length from the front flange to its PCI card bulkhead. G is the space left behind the server for cables: about 152 mm (6 in) for fibre, and roughly 4 in (the guide prints this as 112 mm, though 4 in is 102 mm) for copper Ethernet and power. L is the cabinet's depth.

Intel chassis are designed for 900–1000 mm cabinets. Some fit an 800 mm cabinet but may poke out the back and stop the rear door closing. Try it below.

Why the 51 mm matters

Intel chassis also ship without cable-management arms. The guide points you to the cabinet vendor for those.

5.2.2Slide rail travel (dimension F)

Required

Slide rails telescope to bridge the gap between the front and rear mounting flanges. That gap is F. Most cabinets fix the front flange and let you move the rear one, but each Intel rail kit only stretches over a certain range. If F falls outside it, the rail can't reach both flanges. The guide says this is often what stops an Intel chassis going into a cabinet shallower than 900 mm.

The guide also notes that the kits for the SC5100 and SC5200 use identical rail hardware, which is why their ranges match. (It prints the first of those as "AH2RACK", which looks like a typo for AHD2RACK.)

5.3Cooling

Recommended

Rack servers pull cool air in at the front and push hot air out the back, so the cabinet must let air through in the same direction. No doors at all is best for airflow, but most sites want lockable doors, so the doors have to be secure and breathe at the same time.

Appendix: the PCI card bulkhead

Server length in the depth table is measured to the PCI card bulkhead rather than to the very back of the chassis. The bulkhead is where expansion cards' metal brackets meet the chassis, which is also where the cards' cables plug in, so it's the right reference point for working out cable space behind the server.

Keep in mind. This guide dates from 2005 and its chassis, rail kits and vendor links are long retired. The underlying checks still hold for most rack purchases: confirm the rack follows EIA-310 (now revision E), check flange clearance for your rail kit, size the depth for the server plus cables, keep a gap for the front bezel, and match the door perforation to your cooling.

The guide also lists related standards: the EIA-310-D standard, SSI entry-level electronics-bay and thin power-supply specs, the PCI local bus spec, and IEC 917.
