Will this rack hold an Intel server?

A plain-language, illustrated walkthrough of Intel's Server Rack Cabinet Compatibility Guide: the checklist Intel published so buyers and rack makers could tell whether a 19-inch cabinet would accept its server chassis and rail kits.

The guide boils down to five questions: is the rack built to the EIA-310-D standard, are the mounting holes the right kind, is there room beside the mounting flange for the slide rails, is the cabinet deep enough, and can enough air get through the doors. Each is explained below with a drawing, and the last section lets you test a rack against all of them.

Source: Intel Corporation, Revision 2.4, September 2005, 18 pages.

The compatibility checklist at a glance. Three items are hard requirements; the rest are recommendations.

Two kinds of rack

Information

The guide separates enclosed cabinets from open racks. A cabinet is a free-standing box with four posts, usually with doors and side panels; this is what most data rooms use for servers. An open rack has no walls, and comes in four-post or two-post form. The two-post "relay rack" grew up in the US telecom industry and holds equipment by its front ears only, which is why servers on sliding rails really want four posts.

Check 1

Built to EIA-310-D

Required

EIA-310-D is the 1992 Electronic Industries Association standard behind the "19-inch rack." It fixes the width of equipment front panels, the spacing between the left and right rows of mounting holes, the vertical hole pattern, and the height unit, U, which is 1.75 in (44.45 mm). Intel designs its chassis and rail kits around this standard, and warns that not every rack on the market actually follows it. If a vendor can't confirm compliance, assume the rail kit might not fit.

Key EIA-310-D front dimensions. The PDF's Figure 4 shows these as a drawing; the values here are the standard's published numbers. Each U contains three holes, and the uneven 15.9 / 15.9 / 12.7 mm spacing repeats so you can always find the U boundary.

Check 2

The right kind of mounting holes

Recommended

The standard allows three hole styles on the mounting flange: square, round, or pre-threaded (tapped). Intel's rail kits only work with the first two. Square holes are the best match for Intel's brackets; round holes work too. Tapped holes are out.

Check 3

Room beside the mounting flange

Required

This is the trap the EIA standard doesn't cover. Seen from above, the vertical post with the holes (the mounting flange) has to leave a gap behind it where the Intel slide-rail bracket can sit. Intel needs at least 16.77 mm of that space.

L-shaped flanges usually leave about 22 mm, which is plenty. Many racks ship with U-shaped flanges, whose folded-back lip typically leaves only about 15 mm, too little for the rail. If you aren't sure what a vendor supplies, ask for L-shaped flanges.

Simplified top view of one front corner of the cabinet. The blue block is the space an Intel rail bracket needs; it fits beside the L-shaped flange but collides with the lip of a typical U-shaped one.

Check 4

A deep enough cabinet

RecommendedFront clearance: required

Depth is a sum of three parts, measured front to back. D is the free space between the inside of the front door and the front mounting flange; Intel requires at least 51 mm (2 in) so the bezel and pull handles clear the door and air can circulate. E is the server itself, measured from the flange to its PCI card bulkhead (the rear plate expansion cards bolt to). G is room behind the server for cables: about 152 mm (6 in) for fibre, which bends less readily, or about 112 mm for ordinary Ethernet and power cords.

Intel chassis are designed for 900–1000 mm cabinets. Some will physically go into an 800 mm cabinet, but they may stick out the back far enough that the rear door won't shut. Drag the slider to see how much server length a given cabinet leaves you.

Check 4b

Rail travel between flanges

Required

The telescoping slide rails bolt to both the front and rear mounting flanges, so the distance between those flanges, dimension F, has to fall inside the range each rail kit can stretch to. Front flanges are usually fixed, while rear ones can often be moved, so you can tune F. Intel notes that F is often the limiting factor in cabinets shallower than 900 mm. Move the slider to set your rack's F.

Each bar is the minimum-to-maximum F supported by that chassis's rail kit. Green bars fit at your chosen F. The SC5100 and SC5200 kits use the same telescoping rails, which is why their bars match.

Check 5

Front-to-back airflow

Recommended

Rack servers pull cool air in at the front and push hot air out the back, so the cabinet has to let air travel the same way. No doors at all gives the most airflow, but most sites need lockable doors, so the doors have to be heavily perforated. Intel's target is roughly 63% open area (the "free area ratio") on both front and rear doors. Where extra fans are fitted, mounting them on the rear door is better than on the roof, because they then pull air straight through the servers.

A quick field test: in a 25 mm (1 in) square of door, 63% open area works out to about 33 holes.

Airflow per U that Intel's past products needed, in cubic feet per minute. A cabinet has to be able to move this much air.

Room temperature. Intel servers are rated for 10–35 °C ambient, and the guide suggests 25–30 °C. With a controlled room and doors at 63% open area or more, extra rack fans usually aren't needed.

Chassis the guide covers

Server chassis SC5100, SC5200, SC5300, SR1200, SR1300, SR1325, SR1350, SR2200 and SR2300, plus server platforms SPSH4 and SRSH4.

This guide dates from 2005 and covers long-discontinued hardware. The vendor web addresses it lists have likely changed, and the tested-rack list was never meant to be complete. The underlying checks (EIA-310-D, hole type, flange clearance, depth, rail travel, airflow) are still a sound way to vet any rack.

    Terms used in the guide

    Bezel
    The decorative plastic or metal cover on the front of a server.
    Cabinet
    A free-standing four-post enclosure, usually with doors and side panels.
    Rack
    An open frame for equipment, with two or four posts and no walls.
    Chassis
    The metal structure that holds a server's components.
    CFM
    Cubic feet per minute, a measure of how much air moves.
    EIA-310-D mounting flange
    The front vertical strip with holes that servers, rails and panels bolt to.
    Rear mounting flange
    The matching strip at the back of the cabinet, often movable.
    PCI card bulkhead
    The rear plate inside a server where expansion-card brackets fasten; Intel uses it as the reference point for server length.
    Slide rails
    Telescoping rails that let a server slide out of the rack while staying supported.
    U
    One rack unit of height: 1.75 in, or 44.45 mm.
    IHV
    Independent hardware vendor, here meaning a rack manufacturer.
    FAR
    Free area ratio: the share of a door's surface that is open holes.
