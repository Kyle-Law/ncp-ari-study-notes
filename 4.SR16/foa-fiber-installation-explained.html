    1

    What the guide covers

    The bulletin sets out recommended procedures for installing and testing fiber cabling that carries communications, security and control signals. Its goal is a consistently high-quality installation.

    Scope is premises cabling (inside buildings) plus the outside plant (OSP) portion of a campus, where cable runs between buildings. It builds on the FOA reference books and points readers to the NECA/FOA 301 installation standard, plus companion bulletins on network design, user guidance, restoration and troubleshooting.

      Site requirements and general guidelines

      Receiving and storing materials

      Inspect everything on arrival. If damage is suspected, test for continuity or loss. Confirm quantities, fiber counts, fiber types and cable lengths against the order, and report discrepancies to the supplier. Store everything clean, dry and away from temperature extremes.

      Moving cable reels

      Both cable ends must be accessible on every reel so it can be tested. Small spools move by hand; large reels need lifting equipment or several trained people.

      Pathways, firestopping and grounding

      • Supports first. Install trays, conduit and other support structures before the cable, following TIA/EIA-569 and NECA/BICSI-568, and size them for future growth and bend radius.
      • No shared conduit. Never pull fiber into a conduit or duct that already holds any cable. Subdivide ducts with innerduct instead.
      • Abandoned cable. Remove unused, untagged cable as the National Electrical Code requires, unless the owner reserves it.
      • Firestopping. Seal every penetration of a fire separation with approved firestops. A breached separation often needs watching until repaired; check with the Authority Having Jurisdiction first.
      • Grounding. Fiber itself doesn't conduct, but metal racks, boxes and panels must be grounded per the NEC and TIA-607, and conductive cables need bonding.

      Know your cable

      Premises cables are usually tight-buffered with flame-rated jackets. Outdoor cables are usually loose-tube with water blocking and sometimes armor. Some hybrids work in both places.

      A cable can carry multimode fiber, singlemode fiber or both (a "hybrid"). Identify the type of every fiber before you start, because the two are terminated differently.

      Installing the cable

      Fiber cable can be pulled harder than copper, but only the right way. Too much stress can damage fibers and cause failures later.

      Outdoors, cable can be direct-buried, pulled or blown into duct, or run aerially. Indoors it goes in trays, raceways, hangers, conduit or innerduct. Three ground rules: follow the manufacturer's instructions, check the cable is long enough to avoid splicing, and aim to finish in one pull.

      Pull on the strength members, never the fiber

      Stay under the maximum pulling tension rating. On long runs up to about 5 km (3 mi), use jacket-compatible lubricant and ideally a tension-controlled puller or breakaway eye. Beyond about 4 km (2.5 mi), pull from the middle toward both ends, or pull in stages.

      Bend radius calculator

      Without manufacturer data, the guide's rule is 20× the cable diameter while pulling (the cable is under tension) and 10× once installed.

      Don't twist it

      Vertical runs and cable ties

      • Drop cable down a riser rather than pulling it up, support it often, and leave service loops for gripping and future repairs.
      • Cable ties crush fiber and raise loss. If you use them, hand-tighten just snug enough to slide, then trim the tail so nobody tightens them later. Hook-and-loop ties are preferred.
      • Orange plenum-rated innerduct inside buildings adds protection and makes fiber easy to spot.

      Where fibers end up

      Breakout kits sleeve bare 250 µm loose-tube fibers for connectors. Sealed splice closures protect outdoor splices, buried, in vaults or aerial. Lockable splice and patch panels, racks and cable management keep terminations organized, protected and within bend limits.

      6

      Connectors and splices

      Connectors make a joint you can unplug, for patching and equipment. Splices make a permanent joint. Either way it must have low loss, low back-reflection and protection from its environment.

      Pick connectors that match the cable, the equipment, the loss requirements and the environment, and that have a TIA FOCIS intermateability standard. Multimode is usually connectorized directly in the field; singlemode is more often finished by splicing on factory-made pigtails.

      Verify and keep polarity straight

      Inspect every polished connector under a microscope (FOTP-57) for finish, cracks, scratches and dirt, then loss-test all fibers. Because each direction usually uses its own fiber, duplex connectors are keyed (TIA-568 polarity rules), and simplex connectors must be documented and marked transmit/receive at each end. If total loss is too high, an OTDR tested in both directions (then averaged) pinpoints bad splices.

      Testing the installed cable plant

      Every cable run gets a loss budget during design. After installation, every fiber is tested and its measured loss must be below that budget.

      Reading an OTDR trace

      Tool kit

      OLTS or calibrated power meter and source matched to the fiber and wavelength; good reference test cables (connector loss under 0.5 dB); visual tracer or VFL; 100–200× inspection microscope (video preferred); fiber-specific cleaning supplies; and optionally an OTDR. The relevant FOA standards are FOA-1 to FOA-5.

      8

      Documentation

      Documentation makes testing, upgrades, moves and restoration far easier. Follow ANSI/TIA-606.

      Record the exact path of every fiber in every cable, including each patch panel, splice closure and connector type along the way, plus the insertion loss results and any OTDR traces.
