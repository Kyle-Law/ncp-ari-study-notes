Part 1: Power in, heat out

No raised floor?

Many modern data centers put racks straight onto the concrete slab. Without space under the floor for cold air, they deliver it other ways. Here's how each one works, and how you'd check it.

The goal never changes: cold air to the server fronts, hot air back to the cooling units, and the two never mix. Only the route changes.

Raised floor vs. slab floor

In a raised-floor room, the space under the tiles acts as a giant cold-air duct. On a slab floor there is no such space, so the design leans on two other ideas: bring cold air from above or beside the racks, or cool the air right at the rack, so it hardly travels at all.

Why sites choose slabCheaper to build, holds heavy racks easily, no hidden cable mess under the floor, and suits the newer cooling methods below.
What becomes harderAir can't be "aimed" through floor tiles, so separating hot and cold air relies on containment and careful layout.
Where cables goOverhead, in trays and ladder racks. Keep them from blocking air returns and ducts.
Method 1

Overhead supply (room flooding)

Cooling units along the wall push cold air through ducts in the ceiling, which drop it into the cold aisle. Hot air rises and drifts back toward the cooling units. It's simple, but nothing stops some hot air from falling back into the cold aisle.

Good forSmall rooms and low-power racks, roughly up to 5 kW each.
Watch out forHot spots at the tops of racks and at the ends of rows, where hot air wraps around.
CheckAirflow from each diffuser, and inlet temperatures at the top of racks furthest from the unit.
Method 2

Hot aisle containment

The racks are turned so their backs face each other, and that hot aisle is closed in with doors at both ends and a roof or chimney on top. Hot air is trapped and ducted straight back to the cooling units. The rest of the room becomes one big cold space, so it no longer matters exactly where the cold air comes from. This is the most common slab-floor design.

Good forMost slab-floor rooms. Roughly up to 15–20 kW per rack with good sealing.
Watch out forDoors left open, missing roof panels, gaps under racks, and missing blanking panels. Every leak lets hot air out.
CheckRoom (cold side) temperature, hot aisle temperature (it will be hot, often 35–45 °C, and that's normal), and air pressure between the two sides.
Method 3

In-row cooling

Narrow cooling units sit inside the row, between the racks, looking like just another rack. Each one pulls hot air from the hot aisle behind it and blows cold air into the cold aisle in front. The air only travels a meter or two, so this handles hotter racks well. It's easiest to understand looking down from the ceiling.

Good forMedium to high density, roughly 10–30 kW per rack, and rooms that grow one row at a time.
Watch out forIf one cooler fails, the racks right next to it heat up first. Neighbors must be able to cover.
CheckLoad per row vs. coolers in that row, N+1 per row, and the chilled water or refrigerant supply feeding them.
Method 4

Rear-door heat exchanger

The back door of the rack is swapped for a radiator, like the one in a car, with cold water flowing through it. Hot air leaving the servers passes through the door and comes out already cooled to room temperature. Each rack effectively cools itself, so the room barely notices it.

Good forHigh-density racks, roughly 20–50 kW, and upgrading a few hot racks in an older room.
Watch out forWater near IT equipment. Leak detection and good piping matter. Doors left open bypass the coil.
CheckAir temperature leaving the door (should match the room), water supply temperature and flow rate, and leak sensors.
Method 5

Liquid cooling

Air struggles once a rack goes beyond roughly 30–40 kW, which is common with AI and GPU servers. Liquid carries heat far better than air, so these systems bring liquid right to the hottest parts. There are two main styles.

Good forVery high density: direct-to-chip roughly 30–100+ kW per rack, immersion even more.
Watch out forMost sites are hybrid. Liquid takes most of the heat, but some still goes to the air, so room cooling is still needed.
CheckCDU capacity, coolant temperature and flow, pump redundancy, and how much heat still goes to air.

Which method fits a rack?

The biggest factor is how much power each rack uses, because that's how much heat it makes. Slide to a rack power and see which methods usually suit it. The ranges are rough guides for learning, not design limits.

How to verify: same three steps

The basic method from Part 1 doesn't change. What changes is where you look.

  1. Find the limit.Add up cooling capacity. For in-row coolers and rear doors, capacity lives in each row or rack, so compare each location's load to its own cooling, not just room totals.
  2. Measure server inlet temperatures.Still aim for about 18–27 °C at the server fronts, low, middle and high on each rack.
  3. Plan for one unit failing (N+1).With in-row cooling, ask whether the neighboring coolers can cover. With water-based systems, check spare pumps and chillers too.
