The big idea

A data center is a building full of servers. Every server needs two things to survive: electricity to run, and cooling to carry away the heat it produces.

Almost every watt a server uses turns into heat. So power and cooling are two sides of the same coin. Verifying capacity means answering one question: can this building safely supply the power and remove the heat for all its equipment, including what we want to add next?

Part 1

Power: follow the chain

Electricity doesn't go straight from the wall into a server. It passes through a chain of equipment, and every link has its own limit. Like any chain, the weakest link sets the real limit.

How to check power, in three steps

  1. Find the limit.Read the rating on each piece of equipment, for example "this UPS handles 500 kW" or "this breaker is 30 amps."
  2. Measure what you actually use.Read the live numbers on the UPS screen, PDU display or monitoring software. Use the highest reading over several weeks, not just today's.
  3. Keep a safety margin.Never plan to run at 100%. A common rule is to stay under 80% of a breaker's rating.

Two paths: why "2N" means each side stays under half

Important data centers feed each rack from two completely separate power paths, A and B, like a plane with two engines. If one path fails, the other must carry everything alone. Press the button to see what happens.

Part 2

Cooling: follow the air

If servers use 100 kW of electricity, about 100 kW of heat must be removed. Cooling is often measured in tons, and one ton removes about 3.5 kW of heat. So 100 kW of servers needs roughly 28–30 tons of cooling, plus a little extra.

How to check cooling, in three steps

  1. Find the limit.Add up how much the cooling units can remove (in tons or kW).
  2. Measure temperatures where it counts.The key reading is the air entering the front of the servers. The industry-recommended range (from ASHRAE) is about 18–27 °C. Check low, middle and high on each rack, because the top is usually warmest.
  3. Plan for one unit breaking.If one cooling unit fails, the others should still keep everything within range. This is called "N+1": what you Need, plus 1 spare.

A common beginner surprise: total cooling isn't enough

A room can have plenty of cooling on paper and still overheat some racks, because the cold air never reaches them. The most common cause is a gap in the rack where hot air sneaks back around to the front.

Other common fixes follow the same idea: keep hot and cold air apart. That includes the hot aisle / cold aisle layout shown above, sealing cable holes in the floor, and containment, which means doors or roof panels that close off an aisle entirely.

Worked example

A small server room has servers using 40 kW, one 100 kW UPS, and three cooling units of 5 tons each (about 17.5 kW each).

CheckCalculationResult
Power40 kW ÷ 100 kW = 40% usedComfortable Plenty of room to grow.
Heat to remove40 kW ÷ 3.5 ≈ 11.4 tons, plus a little extra ≈ 12 tonsNeeds about 12 tons
Cooling, all units running3 × 5 = 15 tonsEnough
Cooling, one unit broken2 × 5 = 10 tonsNot enough Short by about 2 tons.
The lesson: the room looks fine on paper, but cooling is the real risk. If one unit breaks on a busy day, the servers overheat. Finding hidden problems like this is exactly what verification is for.

Glossary

kW (kilowatt)
Amount of electrical power, or of heat.
UPS
Uninterruptible power supply. Batteries that keep servers running for the seconds or minutes until the generator starts.
ATS
Automatic transfer switch. Flips from grid power to the generator when the grid fails.
PDU
Power distribution unit. Splits power out to many racks. A rack PDU is the power strip inside one rack.
Ton (of cooling)
About 3.5 kW of heat removed.
CRAC / CRAH
The data center's air conditioners. CRAC has its own compressor. CRAH uses chilled water from a central plant.
N+1
What you need, plus one spare unit.
2N
Two complete, independent systems. Either one can carry the full load alone.
Hot aisle / cold aisle
Rack layout where fronts face fronts and backs face backs, so hot and cold air stay apart.
Blanking panel
A cover for empty rack slots that stops hot air from looping back to the front.
Containment
Doors or roof panels that fully enclose a hot or cold aisle.
PUE
Power usage effectiveness: total building power ÷ IT power. Closer to 1.0 means less energy wasted on cooling and losses.

Where to learn more: the Uptime Institute (known for Tier I–IV data center ratings), the ASHRAE thermal guidelines for data processing environments, and Schneider Electric's free data center white papers, which include many beginner-friendly guides on power and cooling.

Numbers in this guide are simplified for learning. Real sites use detailed measurements, engineering reviews and local electrical codes.
