Liquid to the chip

Direct-to-chip cooling pipes liquid straight onto the hottest parts of a server, the processors. Here's how it works from the chip all the way to the outdoors, and how you'd check it has enough capacity.

~3,500×more heat a liter of water carries than a liter of air
700–1,000+ Wheat from a single modern AI accelerator chip
40–130+ kWper rack in AI clusters, far beyond what air handles well

Why bring liquid to the chip?

Air cooling works by blowing lots of air across heatsinks. As chips got hotter, you'd need so much air, so fast, that fans become loud, power-hungry, and still can't keep up. Beyond roughly 30–40 kW per rack, air starts to struggle.

Liquid solves this because it's so much better at carrying heat. A thin tube of liquid can carry away as much heat as a huge stream of air. So instead of cooling the whole room and hoping the air reaches the chip, you cool the chip itself.

BenefitsHandles very high rack power, fewer and slower fans, less energy for cooling, and warmer water can be used, so chillers run less.
Trade-offsPlumbing inside the white space, leak risk to manage, more parts to maintain, and new skills for the operations team.
Important to knowIt's almost always hybrid. Liquid takes most of the heat (often 70–85%), but memory, power supplies and disks still warm the air.

The cold plate: where it happens

A cold plate is a block of copper that sits directly on top of the chip, replacing the usual air heatsink. Inside are dozens of tiny channels. Cool liquid flows in, picks up the heat through the copper, and flows out warmer.

Two variations you'll hear about:

Single-phase (most common): the liquid stays liquid. It's usually water mixed with propylene glycol and corrosion inhibitors, often called PG25 (25% glycol). Two-phase: a special fluid boils inside the cold plate, absorbing heat as it turns to vapor. It's very effective but less common and more complex.

The two loops: server side and building side

The liquid in the servers never mixes with the building's water. They're kept in two separate loops, joined only through a heat exchanger inside the CDU (coolant distribution unit). This keeps the server loop small, clean and tightly controlled.

Notice the example temperatures. The server loop's supply (30 °C) is a few degrees warmer than the facility water (25 °C). That small gap is called the approach temperature, and every heat exchanger needs some. It also means the facility water must be colder than the coolant you want at the servers.

Chips are happy with surprisingly warm coolant, often 30–45 °C. That's warmer than the outdoor air in many places for much of the year, so heat can often be dumped with dry coolers or cooling towers alone, without running chillers.

Inside the CDU

The CDU is the heart of the system. Think of it as the pump room for the servers' private water supply.

Heat exchangerPasses heat from the server loop to the facility loop without the liquids mixing.
Pumps (usually two or more)Keep coolant moving to every cold plate. A spare pump takes over if one fails.
FiltersCatch tiny particles that could clog the fine channels in cold plates.
Reservoir and sensorsHold spare coolant and measure temperature, pressure, flow and leaks.

CDUs come in two main sizes: in-rack (small, serving one rack) and in-row or floor-standing (large, serving many racks through pipes). Some sites without facility water use a liquid-to-air CDU, which dumps the heat into the room air via a radiator. That's convenient but just moves the heat back to the room cooling.

The one formula you need

Everything about liquid capacity comes down to how much liquid flows and how much it warms up.

Heat removed (kW) = flow (liters per second) × 4.19 × temperature rise (°C) 4.19 is the energy needed to warm one liter of water by 1 °C. For PG25 coolant, use about 4.0.

Example: a 100 kW rack where liquid captures 80%, so 80 kW goes to the coolant. The coolant warms by 10 °C (30 → 40 °C).

Flow needed = 80 ÷ (4.19 × 10) ≈ 1.9 liters per second, which is about 115 liters per minute for one rack.

A handy rule of thumb: with a 10 °C rise, you need roughly 1.5 liters per minute for every kW going into the liquid. Halve the temperature rise and you need double the flow.

How to verify direct-to-chip capacity

Same three ideas as always: find the limit, measure the real load, and keep a margin with a backup. Here's what that means for liquid.

  1. CDU capacity at your temperatures.A CDU's rated kW depends on the facility water temperature and approach. Check its rating at your conditions, not the brochure's best case.
  2. Flow to every rack.Use the formula above to work out the flow each rack needs, then confirm the CDU pumps and pipes can deliver it. Racks furthest from the CDU get the least pressure.
  3. Temperatures.Track coolant supply and return temperature, the temperature rise at each rack, and chip temperatures reported by the servers themselves.
  4. Pressure.Monitor pressure difference across the rack. A slow rise often means filters or cold plates are clogging.
  5. Redundancy.Spare pumps in each CDU, at least one spare CDU (N+1), and spare capacity in the facility water plant.
  6. Leftover air heat.Add up the heat that still goes to air, and check the room cooling can handle it.
  7. Leaks and water quality.Leak sensors under manifolds and CDUs, regular coolant sampling, and filter changes.

Why pumps must never stop

Cold plates hold very little liquid, so there's almost no buffer. If the flow stops, this is roughly what happens:

  1. Pumps stopFlow to the cold plates drops to zero.
  2. Seconds later, chip temperatures shoot upA 1,000 W chip heats very fast with no liquid moving.
  3. Chips slow down or shut offThey throttle to protect themselves, and jobs fail.

That's why CDU pumps are normally on UPS power, just like the servers, and have spares that start automatically.

Glossary

Cold plate
Copper block with tiny channels that sits on a chip and passes its heat into liquid.
CDU
Coolant distribution unit. Pumps, filters and a heat exchanger connecting the server loop to the building loop.
TCS
Technology cooling system. The server-side loop between cold plates and the CDU.
FWS
Facility water system. The building-side loop between the CDU and the chillers or cooling towers.
Manifold
The pair of vertical pipes in a rack (supply and return) that feed each server.
Quick disconnect
A drip-free connector that lets a server be unplugged from the liquid loop for maintenance.
Thermal paste (TIM)
Thermal interface material. A thin layer that fills tiny gaps between chip and cold plate.
Approach temperature
How much warmer the output of a heat exchanger is than the water cooling it.
PG25
Coolant made of water with 25% propylene glycol, plus inhibitors against corrosion and growth.
Capture ratio
Share of a server's heat taken by the liquid. The rest goes to room air.
Single-phase / two-phase
Whether the coolant stays liquid, or boils into vapor inside the cold plate.
ASHRAE W-classes
Industry classes (W17, W27, W32, W40, W45…) naming the maximum facility water supply temperature in °C.

Numbers are simplified for learning. Real designs follow the server maker's coolant, flow and temperature requirements and a mechanical engineer's review. For deeper reading, look at ASHRAE's guidance on liquid cooling and the Open Compute Project's advanced cooling documents.
