Lockout–Tagout in eight steps, explained

Maintenance keeps electrical equipment healthy, but working on a machine is dangerous if energy can come back unexpectedly. Lockout–Tagout (LOTO) is the routine that makes sure a machine is truly dead, stays dead while people work on it, and comes back to life only when everyone is clear.

This page walks through the eight steps from ABB's feature article, with a live diagram of a machine moving through each one.

Source: ABB News, “Lockout-Tagout procedures in 8 steps”, 13 Oct 2025.

    “Energy” means more than electricity

    Step 3 asks you to isolate every source, not just the main switch. The article names five kinds of energy to look for.

    ElectricalUnplug cords, open disconnects, pull fuses
    ChemicalClose supply valves on process lines
    PneumaticClose air valves, vent trapped pressure
    HydraulicIsolate pumps, relieve line pressure
    MechanicalBlock moving parts with safety devices

    Residual energy that survives isolation (step 5)

    Switching off and locking doesn't empty the machine. These can still hurt someone and must be released, relieved or blocked:

    Electric shockAir pressureHeatMotionGravity

    One person, one lock, one key

    Step 4 and step 8 hinge on a simple rule. Everyone working on the machine puts their own lock on each isolation point and keeps the only key. Nobody can remove another person's lock, so the machine can't be switched on until the last worker has taken theirs off.

    The tag doesn't stop anything physically. It tells anyone walking by that the equipment is locked out and who did it.

    Three ways to prove it's dead (step 6)

    Assuming isolation worked is the mistake this step exists to catch. Work starts only after checks confirm there's no energy left.

    Test for voltage

    Use a multimeter at the equipment to confirm there's no voltage present.

    Read the gauges

    Pressure gauges on air or hydraulic lines should sit at zero.

    Try to start it

    Briefly and safely press start. Nothing should happen. Return the control to off afterwards.

    LOTO is a program, not just a checklist

    The article's closing point: the eight steps only work when they sit inside a wider program that's documented, practised and checked.

    A good program protects people during servicing, returns equipment to a safe condition and improves reliability. It also cuts waste: switching equipment off and on correctly avoids damage, downtime, rework and missed production.

    Top 10

    Even so, LOTO (the control of hazardous energy) keeps appearing year after year on OSHA's list of the ten most cited standards, meaning many sites still aren't following it.

    Rules that require it

    Step 1 points out that energy control procedures aren't optional. The article cites three regulations and standards.

    • OSHA 29 CFR 1910.147 (USA)The control of hazardous energy, the core US lockout–tagout rule. osha.gov
    • EU Directive 2009/104/ECMinimum safety and health requirements for work equipment used by workers. eur-lex.europa.eu
    • NFPA 70E, Article 120Electrical safety in the workplace, covering how to create an electrically safe work condition. nfpa.org
