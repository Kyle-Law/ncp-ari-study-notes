Two design philosophies

The article frames the choice as “space first or heat first.” Both modules carry the same kind of payload (8 high-speed electrical lanes feeding lasers and receivers), but they wrap it differently.

OSFP: the ventilated two-room flat

Bigger and taller, with its own heatsink fins built into the module. That extra metal gives it a higher power budget, which the article sees as the key to 800G and beyond. It's a clean-sheet design with no plug-in compatibility with older QSFP gear.

QSFP-DD: the compact high-rise

Almost the same width as the familiar QSFP. “Double density” means a second row of electrical contacts, so it doubles the lanes without getting wider. Older QSFP modules still plug into its ports, but the switch has to supply the cooling.

Top side of each module's edge connector (the bottom side mirrors it). QSFP-DD adds a second, deeper row of pads to the classic QSFP layout: 38 contacts become 76. OSFP uses a single, wider row of 60 contacts.

Spec check. The article says OSFP is “about 14 mm wider and deeper.” Per the MSAs it's roughly 4 mm wider (22.58 vs 18.35 mm), about 18 mm longer (107.8 vs 89.4 mm) and 4.5 mm taller (13.0 vs 8.5 mm), as drawn above and below.

Size, density and heat

A smaller shell lets a switch fit more ports across its front panel; a bigger shell leaves more room for air and fins. The article's summary: QSFP-DD wins on port count, OSFP wins on predictable cooling, and dense QSFP-DD racks may cost more to keep cool.

Lanes and PAM4 signalling

Think of the electrical interface as a highway. Total speed = number of lanes × speed per lane. Both form factors have 8 lanes; what changes between generations is how fast each lane runs.

Why PAM4 doubles the bits

Older links (NRZ) use two voltage levels, so each symbol carries one bit. PAM4 uses four levels, so each symbol carries two bits at the same symbol rate. The catch is that the levels sit closer together, so noise matters more and forward error correction is needed.

Same time window, same number of symbols: PAM4 moves twice the data.

Spec check. The article pairs OSFP with “8 × 50G” and QSFP-DD with “8 × 50G or 4 × 100G,” and says OSFP uses less power per lane. In practice both MSAs define 8 lanes and both have 400G (8 × 50G) and 800G (8 × 100G) generations. Power is set mostly by the optics and DSP inside, not the shell; OSFP's real advantage is that it can shed more heat, not that it draws less.

Compatibility and cost

This is where QSFP-DD's small size pays off. Because its cage keeps the QSFP outline, existing QSFP modules still work in it, so upgrades can be gradual. OSFP needs new switches, and the article describes its vendor ecosystem as smaller, with higher prices and longer lead times.

Power and heat in the rack

The article reports test ranges in which OSFP modules run cooler and draw slightly less than QSFP-DD at the same data rate. Its explanation is thermal: OSFP's larger heatsink keeps the chips cooler, which improves lifespan and reduces HVAC load, while dense QSFP-DD panels can create hot spots that need stronger airflow or even liquid cooling.

Ranges as stated in the article. Real figures vary widely by speed, reach and vendor; check the datasheet.

OSFP heat path

Chip → module's own fins → switch airflow. Cooling travels with the module.

QSFP-DD heat path

Chip → flat module lid → heatsink mounted on the switch cage → airflow. Cooling depends on the host design.

Where each is heading

The article expects both to coexist. OSFP is growing where power and cooling matter most, especially for 800G and terabit-class modules on new platforms. QSFP-DD keeps its hold where density and reuse of existing QSFP gear matter, across hyperscale and enterprise networks, with 400G and 800G parts continuing to arrive.

Case study: a hybrid answer

A hyperscale operator upgrading to 400G weighed both. QSFP-DD's backward compatibility made the near-term migration low-risk; OSFP's thermal advantage was attractive but meant new switch hardware and higher upfront cost. They chose both, each for a different part of the network.

Moving between the two

The article recommends a phased plan and adapters so hardware doesn't have to be replaced all at once:

  1. Test compatibility of the modules and switches you plan to mix.
  2. Check cooling and power budgets for a mixed-form-factor rack.
  3. Stage the rollout, using adapters where needed.
  4. Validate signal integrity and thermal behaviour before committing further.
Spec check. The article says an adapter lets an OSFP module plug into a QSFP-DD port. Commercial adapters work the other way: an OSFP-to-QSFP adapter sits in an OSFP port and accepts a smaller QSFP-family module. An OSFP module can't physically fit a QSFP-DD cage.
