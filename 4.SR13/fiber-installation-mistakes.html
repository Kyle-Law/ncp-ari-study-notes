Two mistakes that quietly ruin fiber links

A visual walkthrough of Belden's field guide on fiber cable installation. The short version: most failed links come from dirty connectors or cables that were pulled the wrong way — and both are avoidable.

Explaining: “2 Big Mistakes to Avoid During Fiber Cable Installation”, Massimo Carboni, Belden blog (Aug 2023).

1

Relaxed cleaning

Skipping or shortcutting endface cleaning. Contamination is the leading cause of fiber link failure.

2

Incorrect pulling

Pulling on the jacket, over-tensioning or over-bending the cable, which damages fibers before the system is even switched on.

Mistake 1: cleaning that's too relaxed

The author, a Belden fiber product engineer, says the industry's advice has always been to keep fiber clean at every step, yet installers under deadline pressure still skip it or cut corners. The consequences listed in the article:

No cleaning raises optical loss. Wrong cleaning can actively degrade the network. Either way you risk excess signal loss, higher reflectance, and even permanent damage to connector endfaces and the active equipment they plug into. Over the life of the network that shows up as lower yield and more service calls.

Based on the article's guidance: dry cleaning handles loose particles, wet cleaning is for oils and stubborn residue and should always be followed by a dry step, and canned air only moves particles around without removing oil.

Shortcuts seen in the field, and what to use instead

The routine: every connector, every time it's mated

The article is explicit that new connectors need cleaning too, and that cleaning happens before every single mating, with an inspection to confirm. That makes it a loop, not a one-off step:

  • Don't use canned air or dusters: they scatter particles and leave oils.
  • Do wear appropriate safety glasses.
  • Don't touch connector endfaces.
  • Do keep protective caps on unplugged connectors.
  • Don't touch the solvent dispensing tip.
  • Do inspect after cleaning, before mating.

Mistake 2: pulling the cable the wrong way

Pulling damage is sneaky: the article points out that small mistakes here may not show up right away, but can cause long-term signal loss or early cable failure. To understand why, look inside the cable.

outer jacket (protection only) aramid yarn — the part built to be pulled buffered fibers

Why the jacket isn't a rope

Most fiber cables carry strength members, commonly aramid yarn, running the full length alongside the fibers. They take the pulling tension so the glass doesn't have to.

The jacket is just a protective sleeve. When installers pull on it, it stretches, and the fibers inside don't stretch with it. The article describes the result: the fibers end up displaced a few feet back from the cable end, termination isn't secure, and the jacket may tear, break or turn wavy.

Dragging cable tightly around corners or against other cables can also friction-burn the jacket.

Wrong pull vs. right pull

Try it: how tight is too tight?

The article warns against bending past the cable's minimum bend radius, which raises attenuation (light leaks out of the core) and can break fibers. It doesn't give a number, so this demo uses a widely used rule of thumb; always check the actual spec sheet for your cable.

Rule of thumb used here (not from the article): about 10× the cable's outer diameter when installed and at rest, about 20× while under pulling tension.

  • Cut back some jacket and pull on the strength members only.
  • Don't exceed the cable's maximum pulling force.
  • Stay above the minimum bend radius the whole run.
  • Don't twist or pinch the cable while pulling.
  • Use proper cable grips, pulleys or sheaves to spread tension evenly.
  • Don't drag it tight around corners or against other cables.

The takeaway

Clean it

Inspect, clean with the right tool, re-inspect, then connect. Every connector, every time. It takes a bit longer and saves far more in failed links and callbacks.

Pull it properly

Let the aramid yarn carry the load, respect the pulling-force and bend-radius limits, and keep the cable from twisting, pinching or rubbing.

The article's broader point: there's a gap between recommended practice and what actually happens on jobsites, and closing it is a way for installers to stand out.
