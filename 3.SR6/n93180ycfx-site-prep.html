Environment

Temperature, humidity, altitude, dust, interference and vibration. Temperature and altitude are linked, so they're explored together.

Temperature and altitude

The switch runs between 0 and 40 °C at sea level. Thin air cools less effectively, so for every 300 m (1,000 ft) of altitude the maximum allowed temperature drops by 1 °C. Switched off, it tolerates −40 to 70 °C.

Humidity

Moisture can get inside and corrode components or change their electrical and thermal properties. Most climate-controlled rooms are fine; in an unusually humid location, add a dehumidifier.

Operating5 – 90 % RH
Non-operating5 – 95 % RH

Dust and particles

The fans that cool the switch also pull in dust. Dust acts as insulation, traps heat and interferes with moving parts. Keep the room clean, and don't smoke, eat or drink near the switch.

Shock and vibration

The switch has been tested for operating, handling and earthquake standards. No special site measures are listed.

Electromagnetic and radio interference

Interference works both ways: the switch can disturb radios, TVs and cordless phones, and strong nearby transmitters can corrupt signals on its cables. RFI is any EMI above 10 kHz, and it can travel over power cables or through the air. The switch meets FCC emission limits; to keep it that way:

  • Cover any open expansion slot with a blank filler plate.
  • Use shielded cables with metal connector shells for peripherals.
  • Prefer twisted-pair cabling with well-distributed grounding conductors, and keep copper runs within the media's maximum length.
Lightning and long runs
If cables exceed recommended distances or run between buildings, a nearby lightning strike can couple enough energy into unshielded conductors to destroy electronics. Bring in surge-suppression and shielding specialists if you've had problems before.

What happens when it gets too hot

Every module has built-in temperature sensors with two thresholds. Here is the sequence the switch follows. Pick where the overheating happens to see the outcome.

Sensors watch continuously

Supervisor, I/O and fabric modules each report temperature against a minor and a major threshold.

Minor threshold crossed: minor alarm

System messages are logged, Call Home and SNMP alerts go out if configured, and fan speed steps up.

Major threshold crossed: major alarm

Same alerts and fan increase, then the switch acts based on where the heat is.

Only that module shuts down

The rest of the switch keeps running.

Failover

The hot supervisor shuts down and the standby supervisor takes over.

2-minute countdown

Temperature is checked every 5 s and messages repeat every 10 s. If it doesn't drop, the switch powers down. Fix the cause, then power it back on.
For this switch specifically
The alarm text is shared across the whole Nexus 9000 family, including modular chassis. The 93180YC-FX is a fixed 1RU switch without separate supervisor modules, so the guide's note applies directly: any major temperature alarm leads to power-down in 2 minutes.

ESD protection and grounding

Static can kill components outright or cause intermittent faults. Voltage spikes and sags can wipe memory. Both are handled by giving the chassis, and you, a solid path to earth.

Path A works when the chassis sits in a grounded rack with clean metal-to-metal contact. Path B is a dedicated cable from the chassis grounding pad to facility ground. The wrist strap clips to the chassis before any hands-on work.

Making the ground actually conduct

  • Strip paint and other coatings where mounting hardware meets the rack.
  • Use thread-forming screws that cut through coatings for metal-to-metal contact.
  • Clean the surfaces and apply an antioxidant compound before assembly.

ESD strap habits

  • Wear a wrist or ankle strap with good skin contact.
  • Clip the leash to the chassis (unpainted metal or the ESD jack) before starting.
  • Check the strap periodically; it should measure 1 to 10 megohms.

Power

Two power supplies share the load with 1-to-1 redundancy. Plan the supply type, how much input to provision, how to feed them, and how to protect each circuit.

Never mix types
Both supplies must be identical. Don't combine AC, DC and HVAC/HVDC supplies in one chassis.

How much power to provision

A supply's rating is not what you budget for. Size the circuit for the switch plus one supply: about 500 W typical, but provision 625 W input to cover peak demand.

The 650 W supply rating (dashed line, right edge) is more than the switch needs. Always base the circuit on switch requirements, not supply ratings.

Feeding the two supplies

In every mode, both active supplies share the load proportionally; one never sits idle. Each circuit feeding the switch should be dedicated to it.

Circuit protection

Airflow

Air must always travel from the cold aisle to the hot aisle. Which end of the switch faces the cold aisle depends on the fan and power supply modules you order, and you can tell them apart by color.

Module colorMeaning
BluePort-side exhaust: air enters at the fans and supplies, leaves at the ports
RedPort-side intake: air enters at the ports, leaves at the fans and supplies
White (HVAC/HVDC supplies only)Dual direction: follows whatever the fan modules do
Keep every module pointing the same way
Fan and power supply modules must share one airflow direction. To change direction, shut the switch down first, then swap the modules. A module blowing the wrong way fights the others and can lead to the thermal shutdown described above.
