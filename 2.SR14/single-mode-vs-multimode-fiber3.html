Single-mode vs multimode fiber

A visual explainer of the DCD industry-view article by Manja Thessin (AFL), October 2024

Dots = light launched at the same instant. In single-mode they stay together. In multimode, steeper paths travel farther and arrive late.

The short version: multimode fiber is still the cheap, easy choice for links inside a rack or row. But because of distance, bandwidth, density and future-proofing, the author argues single-mode is steadily becoming the default for the rest of the data center.

1. It starts with the size of the core

Both fibers have the same outer glass diameter (125 µm cladding). The difference is the light-carrying core in the middle. Single-mode has a tiny ~9 µm core that only allows one path for light. Multimode has a core roughly 5–7× wider, which lets many paths (“modes”) travel at once.

Drawn to scale. The grey disc is the 125 µm cladding in each case; colors follow the standard cable jacket colors (yellow for single-mode, aqua for OM3/OM4).

Why the core size matters: modal dispersion

In a wide core, light bounces along at different angles. A steep ray covers more distance than a straight one, so parts of the same pulse arrive at slightly different times. The pulse smears out. Over a short link this is harmless; over a long one, neighbouring bits blur into each other and the receiver can no longer tell a 1 from a 0. Single-mode fiber has only one path, so this effect essentially disappears and signals can run for kilometres without regeneration.

3. The data deluge and leaf-spine networks

Distance is only half of it. Large data centers use leaf-spine networks, where every leaf switch connects to every spine switch. Individual links may be short, but the combined bandwidth is enormous and links between rows or pods can get long. That is where the author says multimode starts to run out of room.

Single-mode: leaf↔spine, between rows and pods, high aggregate bandwidth Multimode: server↔ToR inside the rack, short and cheap

Can multimode keep up?

OM5 fiber stretches multimode further by carrying several short wavelengths at once (SWDM). The author's view is that this still falls behind the growth curve, while single-mode's bandwidth headroom is effectively unlimited and is the only realistic path to terabit links.

AI and HPC push hardest

AI/ML training clusters and HPC move huge volumes between GPUs, storage and the network. Low latency and high bandwidth directly affect how efficiently that expensive compute is used, which makes these workloads a major driver of single-mode adoption.

4. One fiber, many lanes

Wavelength division multiplexing (WDM) sends several colors of light down one single-mode fiber at the same time. The article's analogy: it turns a single road into a multi-lane expressway, multiplying capacity without pulling new cable.

5. Space and flexibility

Minimum bend radius to scale. Tighter bends mean tighter cable routing in crowded racks.

Data center floor space is costly. The article points to three space and design advantages for single-mode:

Tight bends. Modern bend-insensitive OS2 fiber can bend down to about 10 mm without significant loss, making cable management easier.

Denser connectors. Very small form factor (VSFF) connectors pack more single-mode ports into the same panel space.

A generous loss budget. Low signal loss leaves room to add connectors, splitters and patch points without breaking the link, useful when the layout keeps changing.

Stability. Multimode has gone through OM1 to OM5 with different capabilities, whereas single-mode has stayed consistent, so today's cabling is less likely to be obsolete tomorrow.

7. The author's conclusion

Multimode is not disappearing: it remains a sensible, cost-effective choice for very short links. But the article frames the SMF vs MMF decision as a strategic one about future needs rather than a simple distance-or-price calculation, and argues single-mode is where data centers are heading.

Lean towards single-mode when…

  • links run between rows, pods or buildings
  • you are building leaf-spine fabrics at scale
  • AI/ML or HPC traffic is involved
  • you want the cabling to survive several speed upgrades
  • panel density and tight routing matter

Multimode still makes sense when…

  • connections stay inside a rack or to a top-of-rack switch
  • budget for optics is the main constraint today
  • installers lack single-mode termination tools or experience
  • existing multimode plant already covers the need
