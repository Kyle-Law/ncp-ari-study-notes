How to install fiber optic cable without breaking the light

It covers premises cabling inside buildings plus the outside‑plant runs that link buildings on a campus: planning, handling, choosing cable, pulling, terminating, testing and documenting.

Part 1

What the guide is for

The bulletin sets out recommended procedures for installing and testing fiber cabling that carries communications, security and control signals. Its aim is a repeatable, high-quality installation. It sits inside a family of FOA documents, and it is the "how do we actually install it" piece.

Part 2

Plan before anyone pulls cable

An install coordinates many people and companies, so the guide recommends a checklist that follows the project from design to handover. Here it is as a working checklist in four phases. Ticks are kept only while this page is open.

    Part 3

    Site rules: receiving, handling and building codes

    When the materials arrive

    Inspect everything for damage on arrival, and test for continuity or loss if anything looks suspect. Check quantities against the order, including fiber count, fiber type and cable length. Report and replace anything missing or damaged. Store it all somewhere clean and dry, away from temperature extremes.

    Moving reels

    Small spools go by hand. Big reels need lifting gear or two or more trained people. Both cable ends must stay reachable so the reel can be tested, for example with a visual fault locator and bare‑fiber adapters.

    Pathways, fire and grounding

    Build supports first. Trays, racks and conduit go in before the cable, following TIA/EIA‑569 and NECA/BICSI‑568, sized for future growth and bend radius.

    Never share a duct. Don't pull fiber into a conduit that already holds any cable. Add innerduct to divide an empty duct instead.

    Remove abandoned cable (not terminated and not tagged for future use), as the National Electrical Code requires, unless the owner says to keep it.

    Firestop every penetration through a fire separation with approved compounds or devices. A breach may need to be watched until sealed; confirm rules with the Authority Having Jurisdiction first.

    Ground the metal. Most fiber cable is non-conductive, but racks, patch panels and wall boxes are metal and must be grounded per the NEC and TIA‑607. Conductive (armored) cables need bonding too.

    Part 4

    Know the cable you're installing

    Cables are sorted two ways: by the fiber inside (multimode, singlemode, or a mix called hybrid) and by construction. Indoor cables are usually tight‑buffered with flame‑rated jackets; outdoor (OSP) cables are usually loose‑tube with water blocking and sometimes armor. Hybrid cables need each fiber identified, because multimode and singlemode are terminated differently.

    Flame ratings: which cable goes where

    Every cable inside a building must be listed and carry an NEC 770 flammability marking. An unmarked cable should never go indoors. The letters decode as OF (optical fiber) + N non-conductive or C conductive + the rating.

    Part 5

    Pull it without hurting the glass

    Fiber cable can take more pulling force than copper, but only through its strength members. Stress, tight bends, twists and crush are what kill fibers, sometimes long after the job is done. Outdoors, cable is buried, pulled or blown into duct, or hung aerially; indoors it goes in trays, hangers, conduit or innerduct. Check the cable is long enough for the run and aim to do it in one pull.

    Pull by the strength members

    Bend radius: try it

    A kink or tight bend can break fiber, especially under tension. If the manufacturer gives no figure, the rule is 20× the cable diameter while pulling and 10× once installed and at rest.

    No twists: roll it off, lay it in figure‑8s

    Long outdoor runs

    Up to roughly 5 km (3 mi): use a jacket‑compatible lubricant and ideally an automated puller with tension control or a breakaway eye. Beyond about 4 km (2.5 mi), split the pull.

    Vertical runs and securing cable

    Drop vertical cables down rather than hauling them up. Support them often, with snug ties or Kellems grips, so the jacket isn't carrying the weight. Leave service loops: they help with gripping and give slack for future repairs or reroutes.

    Fiber is sensitive to crushing. Tool‑tightened zip ties cause loss and breakage. Tighten ties by hand so they still slide along the cable, then trim the tail so nobody tightens them later. Hook‑and‑loop (Velcro‑type) ties are preferred.

    Hardware along the route

    Premises cable rides in trays, ladder racks and J‑hooks per TIA‑569. Running it inside bright orange plenum‑rated innerduct adds protection and makes fiber easy to spot. Outside plant goes in conduit or innerduct, direct‑buried, or aerial (lashed to a messenger or self‑supporting). At the ends: breakout kits protect bare 250 µm fibers, sealed splice closures protect outdoor splices, and lockable splice/patch panels in racks organize terminations. Keep bend radius and avoid pinching everywhere, including inside the enclosures.

    Part 6

    Joining fibers: connectors and splices

    There are two ways to end or join a fiber. A connector makes a joint you can unplug, and connects fibers to equipment. A splice makes a permanent joint. Either way it must have low loss, low back‑reflection, and be protected from dirt and damage.

    Choosing a connector

    Styles include ST, SC, LC and MT‑RJ. Whatever you pick must suit the cable, the equipment, the optical loss target and the environment, and must mate with like connectors under a published TIA FOCIS intermateability standard. In practice multimode is usually terminated directly in the field, while singlemode is more often finished by splicing on factory-made pigtails.

    How much loss is acceptable?

    Verify, and get transmit/receive right

    Inspect every polished end face under a microscope (FOTP‑57), then loss‑test every fiber with a power meter and source. If a link fails its loss budget, or the customer requires it, use an OTDR to find the individual splice losses, testing from both ends and averaging, since OTDR readings vary by direction.

    Links normally use one fiber each way, so each end's transmit fiber must reach the other end's receive port. Duplex connectors like duplex SC and MT‑RJ are keyed so they fit only one way; follow the TIA‑568‑B.3 polarity rules. With simplex connectors, document the connections and mark transmit and receive at both ends.

    Part 7

    Test every fiber against its loss budget

    During design, each run gets a loss budget: the expected loss worked out from component specs. After installation you test each fiber and compare. The guide's test sequence:

    1. Inspect and clean connectors. Use a 100–200× microscope (video scopes preferred, and they let you save images). Dust caps don't guarantee clean ends. Clean with fiber-specific supplies only.
    2. Continuity. Trace every fiber end to end with a visual tracer, VFL or OLTS to confirm routing, polarity and that the paperwork matches.
    3. Insertion loss. Test with an OLTS (power meter + source): multimode per TIA‑526‑14 at 850 and 1300 nm, singlemode per TIA‑526‑7 at 1310 and 1550 nm.
    4. Optional OTDR. Verifies installation quality and splices, but must not be used to measure cable loss.
    5. Record length if it isn't already known, from jacket markings, an OTDR, or an OLTS with a length feature.
    6. Troubleshoot and fix anything above expected loss.

    How an insertion loss test is set up

    Reading an OTDR trace

    An OTDR works like optical radar: it fires pulses and plots light scattered back along the fiber against distance. The trace shows length, segment loss, connectors, splices and stress points. Short premises cables can produce confusing artifacts, and the guide stresses that poorly trained OTDR users are the biggest source of testing problems.

    Estimate a loss budget

    A quick calculator for the worst-case figure your measured loss should come in under. The per-km fiber limits are TIA‑568 maximums, which the guide doesn't list, so treat them as a starting point and use your design's own specs if you have them.

    The test kit

    An OLTS or calibrated power meter and source matched to the fiber and wavelengths; good reference cables and mating adapters; a visual tracer or VFL; a 100–200× connector microscope; fiber‑specific cleaning supplies; and, optionally, an OTDR with launch and receive cables. A calibrated meter is the better choice if you also need to check transmitter and receiver power. Relevant FOA standards: FOA‑1 (insertion loss), FOA‑2 (single-ended loss), FOA‑3 (optical power), FOA‑4 (OTDR) and FOA‑5 (datalinks).

    Part 8

    Document where every fiber goes

    Good records make testing, upgrades, moves and restoration far easier. Follow ANSI/TIA/EIA‑606. A backbone cable may carry many links to different places through patch panels and splice closures, so record the exact path of every fiber, every intermediate connection and every connector type, along with its insertion‑loss data and any OTDR traces.

    After handover, the guide's closing checklist applies: inspect workmanship, review test data, bring up the communications system, finish documentation and the restoration plan, and store both along with spare components kept for repairs.
