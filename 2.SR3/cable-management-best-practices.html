Cabling a GPU pod so it stays cool, serviceable and safe

The source page is a short checklist of thirteen rules. They all serve four goals: technicians can reach and swap parts, air moves freely, cable weight never hangs on a connector, and cables are handled gently. This page groups the rules by those goals and shows what each one looks like in a rack.

Same rack, same cables, two outcomes

In the tidy rack, every cable leaves the switch, turns immediately toward a side channel and runs down the rack edge, tied at intervals. The switch face, its transceivers and the equipment below stay unobstructed.

In the messy rack, cables drape across the front of other gear. That blocks the vents that pull in cool air, leaves connectors carrying the weight of the cable, and means pulling one transceiver requires disturbing several others.

Switch between the two views to see which rule each problem breaks. The sections below take the rules one goal at a time.

Keep every part reachable

Switch transceivers and fan units are designed to be hot-swapped. Cabling that gets in the way turns a two-minute swap into a risky untangling job that can disturb working links.

  • Leave replaceable parts accessible. Transceivers and system units such as fan modules must be easy to pull and replace.
  • Choose a rack wide enough. There needs to be room between the switch and the rack's side walls for cables to pass.
  • Never block extraction paths. Nothing should sit in front of a transceiver or module slot.
  • Don't obstruct other equipment. Individual cables and bundles should stay clear of neighbouring gear.
  • Avoid threading cables through pipes or holes. It makes later servicing harder and leaves no room for future runs.

Keep air moving

GPU servers and high-radix switches pull enormous volumes of cold air from the front and exhaust heat at the back. A wall of cable in that path raises inlet temperatures and can push equipment into throttling or thermal shutdown.

  • Don't let cables block airflow. Keep vents and fan intakes and exhausts clear.
  • Remove abandoned cables. Dead cables left in place still obstruct air and can contribute to overheating or shutdowns.

Carry the weight, spare the connector

A connector is a precision optical or electrical interface, not a hook. Large bundles of cable are heavy, and any weight or tension left on a port can damage it or degrade the link over time.

  • Tie cables to the rack structure. This relieves strain and tension at the connector end.
  • Let the support system bear the load. The cable management system and the floor carry the weight. The guide points to its Cable Weight section for planning structured-cabling loads.
  • Support cables at least every 2 m, or lay them in trays.
  • Use trays wherever possible. Trays give continuous support and a defined path for future additions.

Handle cables gently

Optical fibre is sensitive to pinching and tight bends, and connectors are easy to damage by yanking. Two small habits prevent most of this damage.

  • Use soft hook-and-loop ties, not plastic. Plastic zip ties can be overtightened and crush the cable, and have to be cut off to make changes.
  • Unplug by the pull tab. Gently pull the connector's tab to disconnect, never the cable itself.
