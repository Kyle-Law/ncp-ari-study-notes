CRAC with aisle containment, explained

The oldest trick in data center cooling is still the most common one: blow cold air under the floor, push it through the servers, and pull the hot air back. Adding walls between hot and cold air is what keeps it relevant.

How the air moves

A CRAC (Computer Room Air Conditioning) unit sits at the edge of the room and pressurises the space under a raised floor. That cold air only reaches the equipment where it's allowed out, through perforated tiles in front of the racks. Servers pull it in through their front panels, heat it, and exhaust it out the back. The warm air then has to find its way back to the CRAC, usually through a return path in the ceiling.

Why containment matters

Without barriers, the loop leaks. Cold air slips over the tops and ends of rack rows without ever touching a server (bypass airflow), and hot exhaust curls back around into the intakes (recirculation). The CRAC ends up working harder to cool a lukewarm mix. Containment is simply physical separation, and it comes in two flavours.

Cold aisle containment

  • Encloses the space where cold air is supplied, usually with a roof and end doors.
  • Stops cold supply air from mixing with the rest of the room.
  • The rest of the room becomes the hot return space.

Hot aisle containment

  • Encloses the server exhaust side instead.
  • Sends hot air directly back to the CRAC units, typically via the ceiling.
  • Captures heat more completely, which is why the article calls it usually the more effective option.

What each setup can handle

The article's performance table, drawn as ranges. Each step of containment roughly doubles to triples the supportable rack density, and pulls PUE (total facility power divided by IT power) closer to the ideal of 1.0.

The article's intro puts the practical ceiling at around 10–12 kW per rack, while its table stretches hot aisle containment to 15 kW. Read 15 kW as a best case in a well-tuned room, not a planning number.

Where it shines, where it runs out

CRAC with containment isn't chosen for peak performance. It wins on being familiar, cheap to improve, and easy to keep running.

Simple and reliable

  • Decades of field experience, with well-understood failure modes.
  • Serviced with ordinary HVAC skills and readily available parts.
  • A large ecosystem of suppliers and service providers.

Easy to retrofit

  • Containment can be added without major construction.
  • Works with existing raised floors and building management systems.
  • Allows improvement in steps rather than a full replacement.

Cost-effective

  • Less bypass air means less wasted cooling energy.
  • Lower capital cost than advanced cooling technologies.
  • Little extra staff training required.

Physical limits of air

  • Airflow volume: there's only so much air a unit can move, which caps density.
  • Static pressure: keeping enough underfloor pressure gets harder as loads climb.
  • Heat transfer: air carries heat far less effectively than liquid.
  • Hot spots: uneven distribution creates temperature variation across the room.

What the building must provide

  • Raised floor: the underfloor plenum is the delivery system.
  • Ceiling return: enough space for hot air to find its way back.
  • Electrical capacity: CRAC units draw significant power themselves.
  • Floor space: perimeter units eat into usable white space.

Who still uses it

Traditional enterprise data centers

Sites with existing infrastructure and moderate density can add containment and get more efficiency out of what they already own, without disruption.

Colocation facilities

Standardised operation and simple maintenance suit multi-tenant halls, as long as tenant densities stay in the traditional range.

Hybrid cooling rooms

CRAC handles the background heat load while liquid cooling is targeted at a few high-density racks.

Budget-conscious builds

When cost control and familiar procedures matter more than maximum density, it delivers dependable cooling.

Getting the most out of it

Design

  • Model airflow with CFD to check distribution and temperature uniformity.
  • Prefer hot aisle containment where possible.
  • Place perforated tiles to match where servers actually draw air.
  • Give return air enough ceiling space and a clear route.

Containment build

  • Seal every gap; small leaks bring bypass airflow back.
  • Plan maintenance doors and emergency exits.
  • Coordinate with fire suppression systems.
  • Fit temperature and pressure sensors to prove it's working.

Operations

  • Keep up filter changes, coil cleaning and mechanical checks.
  • Track temperature, humidity and energy continuously.
  • Tune setpoints to real loads, not worst-case guesses.
  • Respect the density ceiling when placing new equipment.

Upgrading without starting over

The article frames CRAC as a platform to build on. Improvements come in two tiers: tune the existing system first, then add other cooling technologies where density demands it.

Starting point

CRAC with containment

  • Raised floor supply
  • Hot or cold aisle enclosure
Incremental improvements

Make the air system smarter

  • Smart sensors and automated control
  • Variable speed fans matched to demand
  • Better sealing and airflow management
  • Spot cooling for dense zones
Hybrid integration

Add other cooling where needed

  • Direct-to-chip liquid or rear-door heat exchangers for dense racks
  • In-row coolers for persistent hot spots
  • Free cooling (economisers) to cut compressor use
  • Heat recovery to warm the building

The economics, and what comes next

On the capital side, CRAC is usually cheaper up front than advanced cooling, reuses the raised floor and electrical systems already in place, and lets containment spending be phased over time. On the operating side, standard HVAC servicing, commodity spare parts and familiar procedures keep running costs down, while containment delivers measurable energy savings.

Looking ahead, the article expects CRAC to keep evolving through AI-driven control and predictive maintenance, better heat exchangers and fans, and tighter coordination with other cooling systems. Its likely roles are background cooling in hybrid rooms, continued service in established facilities, a bridge during gradual modernisation, and the sensible choice where budgets are tight.

Good fit

Existing raised-floor rooms, racks mostly under about 10–12 kW, teams that value simplicity and predictable maintenance.

Look elsewhere

AI and other high-density racks, rooms without floor or ceiling plenum space, or plans that need well beyond air's heat-carrying limits.
