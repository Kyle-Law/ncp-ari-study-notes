Verifying power redundancy per rack

A rack is redundant only if losing any one power path keeps every device running, and the surviving path can carry the whole load. Both halves of that have to be checked.

Exam objectiveVerify power redundancy per rack.

In one minute

  • Typical design: 2N at the rack: two PDUs (A and B) fed from independent upstream paths (different panels, UPSs and ideally utility sources).
  • Every dual-corded device: PSU 1 → PDU A, PSU 2 → PDU B. Both cords on the same PDU is the classic hidden single point of failure.
  • Capacity: either side alone must carry the full rack. So total rack load ≤ 80% of one feed's capacity, which means each side runs at under ~40% in normal operation.
  • Device-level redundancy matters too: DGX H100 has six 3.3 kW PSUs as 4+2 (≈10.2 kW max). GB200 NVL72 has 8 × 33 kW power shelves as N+N (two halves, each able to run the rack).
  • Single-corded gear needs an ATS (automatic transfer switch) to get A/B protection.
  • Verify by tracing (labels, circuit IDs), monitoring (PSU status in the BMC, per-PDU load), and a controlled feed-pull test in a maintenance window.

The vocabulary

N

Exactly the capacity you need. No spare: any failure drops load.

N+1

One spare module or unit beyond need (pumps, CRACs, UPS modules). Survives one component failure, but shares one path.

2N

Two complete, independent systems. Either one can carry the full load alone. Survives losing an entire path.

N+N

Used for device PSUs/power shelves: N supplies on feed A, N on feed B. Lose a whole feed and N remain.

4+2

DGX H100: 4 PSUs carry the load, 2 are spare. Split across feeds so losing one feed still leaves 4 or more.

How to verify a rack

  1. Trace the pathsPDU A and PDU B labels show their upstream panel/circuit IDs. Confirm they come from different panels, breakers and UPSs, not two circuits off the same panel.
  2. Check every cordEach dual-corded device has one cord on A and one on B. Colour-coded cords (e.g. one colour per feed) make this visible at a glance.
  3. Check device PSU redundancyAll PSUs present and healthy in the BMC/IPMI or tools like nvsm show health on DGX. Know each platform's scheme (4+2, N+N) and how PSUs map to feeds.
  4. Check failover capacitySum the rack's real peak load. It must be ≤ 80% of one feed's rating (and per phase, see 1 · T3). On N+N systems, enable power capping if the guide calls for it.
  5. Handle single-corded gearPut it behind an ATS PDU fed from A and B, or accept and document the risk.
  6. Test (planned)In a maintenance window, switch off feed A at its breaker and confirm nothing drops and no alarms beyond "redundancy lost". Restore, then repeat for B.

Exam traps

  • Two PDUs on the same upstream circuit.That looks like A/B but it's one path. Verify upstream independence.
  • Both cords in PDU A.The device survives a PSU failure but not a feed failure.
  • Each side at 60%.Fine day to day, but after failover the survivor sits at 120% and trips. In 2N each side must stay under half of the 80% limit.
  • N+1 ≠ 2N.N+1 covers a failed module, not a lost path.

Check yourself

1. A rack's A and B PDUs each show 9 kW on 17.3 kW-rated feeds. Is it safe to lose a feed?

After failover one feed carries the sum, 18 kW, which exceeds the feed. Total rack load must stay ≤ 80% of one feed.

2. What does "2N" mean?

2N duplicates the entire path, which is why it survives losing a whole feed.

3. How is a DGX H100's power supply redundancy described?

Six 3.3 kW supplies, up to ~10.2 kW system draw. Four carry the full load and two are spare.

4. A single-corded switch must survive loss of either feed. What do you use?

An ATS switches the single cord to the surviving source. Never bridge two feeds with a splitter.
