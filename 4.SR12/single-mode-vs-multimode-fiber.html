Single-mode vs multimode fiber

Both carry data as light through glass. The difference comes down to how wide the glass core is, and that one dimension decides how far the signal can go, what lights it, and what it costs.

Why fiber at all

Fiber optic cable sends data as pulses of light instead of electrical current on copper. That buys three things: much more bandwidth, long distances before the signal needs help, and immunity to the electromagnetic interference that disturbs metal cables.

Those properties make fiber the backbone of the internet, streaming, cloud services and IoT networks. Within fiber there are two main families, and they are not interchangeable.

The one dimension that matters: core diameter

Single-mode has a tiny core of roughly 8.3–10 µm. It is so narrow that light can only travel one way: straight down the middle. With only one path, a pulse stays sharp, and the signal can go very long distances.

Multimode has a core of 50 or 62.5 µm, five to seven times wider. It is easier to launch light into, but that light spreads into many paths (“modes”), which limits how far a fast signal can travel cleanly.

Extra context: both types share the same 125 µm outer cladding, so they look identical to the eye. Cable jackets give it away by convention: yellow for single-mode, aqua (OM3/OM4) or orange (OM1/OM2) for multimode. This page borrows those colors.

Why more paths means less distance

Data is a stream of on/off light pulses. In multimode fiber, parts of each pulse take routes of different lengths, so they arrive at slightly different times. This is modal dispersion: every pulse gets wider the further it travels.

Eventually neighbouring pulses overlap and the receiver can’t tell a 1 from a 0. That caps the useful bandwidth over distance. Single-mode fiber has only one path, so this effect essentially disappears.

Different lights, different wavelengths

Single-mode needs a laser: a tight, coherent beam is the only way to aim light into a 9 µm core. It runs at 1310 nm and 1550 nm, the infrared windows where glass loses and spreads the signal least.

Multimode can use cheaper, less precise sources: LEDs or VCSELs (vertical-cavity surface-emitting lasers). It runs at 850 nm and 1300 nm, suited to short links in buildings and data centers.

How far each one reaches

The article places multimode at runs under about 2 km, and single-mode at up to around 100 km before the signal needs amplifying, at data rates of tens of Gbps. The scale below is logarithmic, because the gap is enormous.

Cost: where the money goes

Single-mode generally costs more to deploy. Its narrow core demands precise manufacturing, and it needs laser light sources, which are pricier than LEDs. Multimode uses simpler parts and is the cheaper choice for short runs.

The article’s advice is to weigh the upfront price against the job: over long distances, single-mode’s reach and bandwidth usually justify the extra spend.

Extra context: in current deployments the bare single-mode cable is often no more expensive than multimode. The price gap mostly lives in the transceivers at each end.

Why you can’t mix them

The two types aren’t interchangeable. Equipment is built for one core size and one kind of light source, so plugging the wrong fiber in gives a weak or unusable link.

The geometry shows why: light coming out of a 50 µm multimode core hits a 9 µm single-mode core, and almost all of it misses.

Where each one lives

Single-mode

  • Telecom backbones between cities and countries
  • Undersea cable systems
  • High-speed broadband, internet and cable TV networks

Multimode

  • Inside data centers, between racks and rows
  • Office and corporate LANs
  • University and school campus networks

Looking ahead, the article points to research like photonic crystal fibers for single-mode (higher rates, lower loss), and to better light sources and fiber designs that could push multimode’s bandwidth and reach further.
