The chapter isn't about network topology as such; it's about the physical consequence of that topology: hundreds of cables per scalable unit that need trays wide enough and supports strong enough to carry them.

What this chapter covers

The page deliberately narrows its scope. Network architecture (fabrics, switch models, connectivity) lives in the separate DGX SuperPOD Reference Architecture, and detailed cabling practice lives in NVIDIA's Cabling Data Centers guide. This chapter only discusses the network where it touches the building: space, weight and pathways.

So the whole page reduces to one question for a facilities team: given how many cables this cluster needs, how big and how strong must the overhead cable pathways be?

Two kinds of cable, very different footprints

For planning, the guide gives average figures for the two cable types (Table 17). Use them to size trays, ladder racks and strain reliefs early on, then redo the math with the actual spec sheets once the cables are chosen.

The comparison on the right is the real takeaway: a copper cable occupies about 10× the tray cross-section of an AOC cable and weighs 5× as much per metre. That's why fiber (active optical cable) dominates the long runs across the data hall.

Six things to pre-plan

Cables concentrate heavily in two places: the central management racks and the overhead pathways above them. The guide lists the areas of analysis that must be settled before installation:

  • Top-of-rack penetrationsOpenings in rack roofs where cable bundles drop in.
  • Cable tray typeLadder, wire-basket, solid-bottom and so on.
  • Tray width and depthSets the usable cross-sectional area A.
  • Tray fill ratioHow much of A cables may occupy; the guide says stay at or below 50%.
  • Structural loadCable + tray + support weight, checked against anchors.
  • Cable management devicesStrain reliefs, waterfalls, bend-radius guides.

Where the cable counts come from

The compute fabric is a two-tier leaf–spine InfiniBand network. It grows in scalable units (SUs) of 32 DGX H100 systems, each with 8 GPUs and 8 compute network ports. Every SU adds 8 leaf switches, and every leaf connects up to every spine.

Overhead pathways and their supports

The guide's Figures 18 and 19 show front and rear views of a typical overhead cable management system above one SU (see them on the original page). The schematic here captures the support rules that go with it:

Straight sections are typically supported every 5 ft (1.5 m). Curved sections may need extra supports, and any span longer than 5 ft should be evaluated.

Splices, joints and height transitions need a support within 1 ft (0.3 m).

Supports and their anchors are evaluated separately. Each must carry cables, tray and support structure combined.

The tray fill formula

To find how many cables a tray holds, divide the usable area by the area of one cable's circular cross-section:

N = (F / 100) × A ÷ [ π × (D / 2)² ]

where A is the inside tray area (in²), D the cable diameter (in), F the fill ratio (%), and N the number of cables.

The guide's worked example

A 3 mm AOC cable is 0.118 in across, so it takes up π × 0.059² ≈ 0.01096 in². A tray of 11.5 in² (7,419 mm²) at 50% fill gives 0.5 × 11.5 ÷ 0.01096 ≈ 524 cables, just enough for the 508 cables in one SU (252 node + 256 spine–leaf). That's why the guide calls 11.5 in² the minimum.

Why 50%? Cables don't pack perfectly, and trays need headroom for airflow, bend radius, future adds and pulling new cables without damaging existing ones. The guide advises never filling beyond 50%.

Structural load

The guide's reference figure for one SU: 508 fiber cables × 0.009 kg/m = 4.57 kg (≈ 10 lb) per linear metre of pathway, from cables alone. The tray and its hangers add to this, and it multiplies with every SU sharing the same run.
