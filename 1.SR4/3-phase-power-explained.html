An illustrated walkthrough of “3-Phase Power Explained” by Synaccess Networks

Three runners keep the lights on

Picture a dark room with treadmills in it. Each runner wears shoes that flash when they hit the belt. One runner gives you flickering light with dark gaps between steps. Three runners, staggered so a foot is always landing, keep the room lit. That's the difference between single-phase and three-phase power.

From the grid to the rack

Generators produce alternating current, and it travels across the grid and into industrial buildings as three-phase power. A data center then has to decide how to hand that power to the equipment in each rack. There are two basic choices.

Peak voltage vs. the number on the label

In a service, each hot wire swings between roughly + V and − V relative to neutral, times a second. Nobody calls it “ volts,” though. AC is described by its RMS value: the steady DC voltage that would deliver the same power to a load.

For a sine wave, RMS is the peak divided by √2. So V peak ÷ 1.414 ≈ VAC.

Single-phase: one runner, two shoes

Back to the runner. Let the positive half of the wave be the left shoe and the negative half the right shoe. At each peak, either shoe lights the room equally well. Polarity doesn't matter; both halves of the cycle deliver usable energy.

The catch is the moment in between, when the voltage crosses zero. For an instant, neither shoe is down and the room is dark.

A single-phase rack PDU is built for exactly this: it takes one line and neutral, and wires every outlet line-to-neutral. Plug anything in and it runs at VAC.

Three-phase: three runners, a third of a cycle apart

A three-phase system has three hot wires, and each one measures VAC to neutral. What makes it three-phase is timing: each wire's wave is shifted by a third of a cycle (120°) from the next.

On the left, each wire is an arrow spinning around the circle. The height of an arrow's tip is that wire's voltage at this instant, and the dotted lines carry it across to the waveforms.

Why the room never goes dark

Whenever one wave passes through zero, the other two are well away from it. Feed equal loads from all three, and the total power they draw doesn't pulse at all: it stays perfectly flat. A single-phase load, by contrast, gets power in bursts that fall to zero twice every cycle.

Line-to-line: how a delta PDU gets more voltage

A three-phase rack PDU takes all three phases in and splits them into three groups of outlets. Many are wired in a delta configuration: instead of connecting each outlet from a hot wire to neutral, it connects them between two hot wires.

In the analogy, that's two runners whose shoes are lit at the same moment, so the room gets brighter. Electrically, the outlet sees the difference between two waves that are 120° apart, and that difference is √3 times bigger: V × 1.732 ≈ V. Higher voltage means the same power at less current, which is more efficient for the equipment.

In 230/400 V countries like Malaysia, three-phase rack PDUs are usually wired in wye (star) instead: each outlet group sits between one hot wire and neutral and gets 230 V, because 400 V line-to-line is above what IT power supplies accept. What that buys you at the rack

Power is voltage times current, and breakers limit the current. So the way a circuit is wired decides how much equipment one feed can run. At the same current rating, a three-phase feed delivers √3 × VL–L × I: three times what a single V circuit can.
