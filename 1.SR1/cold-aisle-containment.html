Keep the cold air cold

How cold aisle containment stops server exhaust from leaking back into the air your servers breathe, and the groundwork a data hall needs before the panels and doors go up.

Why this matters

More data processing means more heat, and every watt of heat has to be removed by cooling equipment that itself burns electricity. Aisle containment, hot or cold, has become the default way to cut that overhead, because it lets the cooling plant deliver exactly the air the servers need instead of fighting a room full of mixed air.

Where the conventional layout leaks

In a typical hall, CRAC units sit around the perimeter, outside the rack rows, and blow cold air into the plenum under a raised floor. Racks are arranged in the hot aisle/cold aisle pattern: facing rows share a cold aisle for their intakes, and back-to-back rows share a hot aisle for their exhaust.

The pattern is good practice, but nothing physically separates the two air streams. Hot exhaust drifts over the tops of the racks and wraps around the ends of each row, so the servers at the top and ends of a row breathe unpredictable, sometimes dangerously warm air. Pushing more cold air down the aisle masks the problem at the cost of more fan energy.

Groundwork before containment

Containment works best in a hall that is already tight and tidy. These measures come first, in roughly this order, and the last step is the containment itself.

  1. Seal the room

    Give the space a good vapour barrier, close leaks around doors, windows and other openings, and keep outside air to a minimum.

    Server heat is entirely sensible heat: it raises temperature but adds no moisture. Any humidity that leaks in adds a latent load that eats cooling capacity without cooling a single server.

  2. Optimise the airflow path

    Seven detail-level fixes that keep cold air on its way to server intakes and hot air on its way back to the CRACs. Figure 3 keys each one to where it happens in the room.

  1. Raise the supply temperature

    Many halls run colder than they need to. Raising supply air toward the top of the ASHRAE recommended range reduces the energy spent on cooling while keeping equipment within guidelines.

  1. Keep procedures current

    Methods of procedure (MOPs) and other process documents for maintenance, new equipment installs and routine operation should reflect energy-efficiency practice and be followed to the letter. One missing blanking panel after an install undoes the work.

  2. Monitor the system

    Regular monitoring flags preventive maintenance, such as filter changes, before a drifting system starts wasting energy.
