NVIDIA cables vs. everyone else's

A third-party cable often fits the port and the link often comes up. The difference is that NVIDIA has tested its own LinkX cables with the NIC, firmware and switch at each end, and supports them if anything goes wrong.

Exam objectiveDescribe the difference between NVIDIA cables vs. others.

In one minute

  • LinkX is NVIDIA's (formerly Mellanox's) line of DAC, ACC, AOC cables and optical transceivers, designed and tested together with ConnectX adapters, BlueField DPUs and Quantum/Spectrum switches.
  • Each firmware release publishes a validated cables list. NVIDIA parts on it are supported. Third-party parts may appear as "seen to work", but come with no performance guarantee, and problems with them aren't handled through normal support.
  • At 100G-per-lane PAM4 (NDR) and 200G-per-lane (XDR), signal margins are thin. The NIC, cable and switch have to be tuned together, which is why end-to-end validation matters more than at older speeds.
  • NVIDIA parts are identifiable by part number (legacy OPN like MCP4Y10-N001 or 980-series P/N), by the module EEPROM the switch/NIC reads, and by physical details like finned vs flat-top OSFP.

What "validated" actually covers

A link is three things working as one system: the transmitter in one port, the cable or optics, and the receiver in the other port. NVIDIA tunes and tests the whole chain, including firmware settings like equalization and FEC. A third-party cable is only one piece of a chain that nobody tested as a whole.

The LinkX family at a glance

Know these media types and the legacy part-number prefixes that go with them. On the exam and on site, the prefix is the fastest way to know what you're holding.

MCP…

Passive copper (DAC)

Just wire and connectors. Cheapest, lowest power, lowest latency. Shortest reach: about 0.5–3 m at NDR.

MCA…

Active copper (ACC)

Copper with signal conditioning in the ends. Stretches copper to about 4–6.5 m at NDR.

MFA… / MFS…

Active optical cable (AOC)

Optics sealed into both ends with fiber between. Plug-and-play, no cleaning of separate connectors.

MMA…

Multimode transceiver

850 nm VCSEL optics for short fiber runs (tens of metres).

MMS…

Single-mode transceiver

1310 nm optics for 100 m up to 2 km (and longer LR/ER parts).

MFP7E…

Passive fiber cable

The patch cable between two transceivers. MPO-12 with APC (angled) end-faces in the NDR family.

Reach figures are from the ConnectX-7 validated-cables list. Always check the datasheet for the exact part.

Physical details that are NVIDIA-specific

NVIDIA's NDR/XDR switches use a twin-port OSFP cage, meaning one plug carries two independent 400G links (800G total at NDR). The top of the plug has to match how the cage is cooled:

Finned top (IHS)

Integrated heat sink on the plug. For switch cages, which are open on top so the plug brings its own cooling.

Flat top (RHS)

For cages that carry a riding heat sink: ConnectX-7 OSFP adapters (single-port) and systems like DGX (twin-port, flat).

Splitter / breakout

One twin-port switch end fanning out to 2×400G or 4×200G adapter ends. Descriptions say e.g. "flat to finned", and suffixes like -FLT / -FTF.

Don't confuse "fits" with "right"

A finned plug won't seat under a riding heat sink, and a flat plug in an open switch cage has no cooling of its own. Ordering the wrong top style is a classic deployment error.

How to tell on site

  1. Read the labelCheck the OPN or 980-series P/N against the firmware release notes' validated list for the NIC/switch firmware you're running.
  2. Read the module EEPROMEvery cable/transceiver reports vendor name, part number and serial number. On a host, NVIDIA's mlxlink -d <device> -m prints module info. On the switch, the transceiver/interface "show" commands do the same.
  3. Validate the fabricThe Cable Validation Tool (CVT, in UFM) compares the cabling against the intended topology and flags wrong or unsupported cables. There's a free self-paced CVT course among the exam readings.
  4. Check lifecyclePrefer GA parts. LTB and EOL parts are still validated if you own them, but you can't count on buying replacements.

Exam traps

  • "Third-party cables are unsupported, so they won't link up."Wrong. Many work. The difference is validation, guarantee and support, not whether the link comes up.
  • "Same form factor means interchangeable."Fit isn't validation. Also check top style (finned vs flat), twin vs single port, and protocol (IB vs Ethernet qualification).
  • Mixing up prefixes.MCP = passive copper, MCA = active copper, MFA/MFS = AOC, MMA = multimode, MMS = single-mode transceiver.
  • QSFP-DD on an NVIDIA adapter.Doesn't exist. QSFP-DD is used only on Ethernet switches. InfiniBand and ConnectX/BlueField don't use it.

Check yourself

1. A third-party 400G cable links up fine between a ConnectX-7 and a Quantum-2 switch. What is the main risk?

Third-party parts can work, but NVIDIA gives no performance guarantee for them, and issues with them go through a feature-request process instead of normal support.

2. Which prefix identifies an NVIDIA passive copper DAC?

MCP = passive copper. MCA = active copper, MMS = single-mode transceiver, MFA = active optical cable.

3. Which OSFP top style goes into a Quantum-2 switch cage?

Switch cages are open on top, so the plug must bring its own heat sink: twin-port, finned. Flat tops are for cages with a riding heat sink (adapters, DGX).

4. Where do you check whether a specific cable is validated for your ConnectX-7?

Each ConnectX firmware release lists the validated NVIDIA cables, third-party cables seen to work, and tested switches.
