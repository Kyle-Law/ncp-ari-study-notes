Six attributes, two kinds of trouble

The paper names six traits of AI workloads. They sort neatly into two groups: three that pack more power into each rack, and three that make that power behave badly.

Drive rack density past 100 kW

AI workloads: training vs. inference

Training spreads one job across many accelerators running near full utilization for hours to months. Because progress is saved in checkpoints, a power loss means resuming, not starting over, so UPS redundancy matters less.

Inference answers user queries with compressed models. Hardware per model ranges from one server to several racks, and server count grows with users. It is often business-critical, so resiliency matters more.

Accelerator network communication

Every GPU gets its own network port, and GPUs compute faster than they can talk to each other, so the network is the bottleneck. Short copper links inside a rack are cheap and fast; optical links between racks cost roughly ten times more and draw more power. The result: a strong incentive to cram as many accelerators into one rack as possible.

Thermal design power (TDP)

Each GPU generation draws more power to do more operations. Accelerators account for roughly half of a cluster's power. See the TDP table below.

Create power anomalies

Peak power of accelerators

GPUs can briefly overshoot their TDP (by around 50%) for tens of milliseconds, then drop to idle, many times per second. That higher ceiling is called the electrical design point (EDP). Software power capping and smoothing can tame it somewhat.

Synchronous computation

Ordinary servers peak at random times, so their peaks average out and designers can safely oversubscribe UPSs and chillers. A training cluster's GPUs peak together, acting like rapid step loads on the whole electrical system. That's the slider above.

AI cluster size

Training a large model can fill an entire data center. Even a modest cluster can be a big share of a facility's load, and at high densities that concentration is hard to host.

Density is climbing fast

Most traditional facilities were built for about 10–20 kW per rack. The paper's trend chart shows AI compute racks heading toward the megawatt range by the end of the decade.

Power: from the grid to the rack

Getting the electricity at all

Because training can't be split across distant sites without a latency penalty, AI campuses need hundreds of megawatts in one place. In energy-constrained regions, grid connection queues run 3–5 years. Developers have to plan sourcing early, including the option of on-site prime power generation.

Distributing it inside the building

Internal power systems must cope with peaky loads, step loads and higher arc flash hazards. Older North American sites still distributing at 120/208 V will need higher voltages, and power "blocks" must grow to feed many 100 kW racks. Even new designs need updating.

Cooling: why liquid wins

Air can cool a hot chip, given a tall enough heat sink. The problem is that tall heat sinks make tall servers, and tall servers mean fewer GPUs per rack, which fights the networking incentive to pack GPUs together. Liquid cold plates are under an inch tall. The paper's example, in a 42U rack:

32 GPUsAir-cooled: 4 × 10U servers
168 GPUsLiquid-cooled: 21 × 2U servers

The catch

Direct-to-chip cooling needs tightly controlled coolant temperature, flow and chemistry. Chiller water can't go straight through a cold plate (it would corrode and foul it), so a coolant distribution unit (CDU) keeps the two loops separate.

No standards yet

Chipmakers, server OEMs, cooling vendors, engineers and operators haven't converged on coolant properties, interfaces or dimensions. That means galvanic corrosion risks, custom (costly) systems, mixed requirements, and air-cooled gear that must coexist. The advice: do a design assessment before deploying, and lean on partners' experience and modeling tools.

Raised-floor tile alignment no longer matters, since AI halls use the floor for pipes and cables rather than airflow. Have capacities validated by an independent third party (the paper suggests UL and ISTA), and remember racks outlive the IT inside them.

Hot power cords. Standard C19/C20 connectors are rated to 65 °C. In hot, dense racks, specify high-temperature C21/C22 (155 °C) and monitor rear-of-rack temperatures through DCIM.

Supply chain, software and services

Reduce schedule risk

Lead times are long, so design infrastructure in parallel with the IT stack and order early. The paper recommends building from standard sub-assemblies and prefabricated modules, choosing suppliers with a global manufacturing footprint, consolidating to fewer partners, checking supply-chain rankings, and involving supply specialists during design.

Reduce design and operating risk

"Eyeballing" a retrofit doesn't work at hundreds of kilowatts. Use EPMS and DCIM to learn real peak draws, then run capacity, protection coordination, arc flash and short-circuit studies. Digitalized single-line diagrams and digital twins make these studies faster and let you test changes before making them. Service contracts can offload this work when staff are scarce.

What's coming

Medium voltage in the IT space

Running ~13 kV closer to the load cuts copper and removes a transformer stage.

Solid state transformers

Small, light power electronics that convert between AC and DC.

Solid state circuit breakers

Semiconductor switches open far faster than mechanical ones, limiting arc flash energy.

Sustainable dielectric fluids

Possible successors to water-glycol if they improve heat transfer and avoid PFAS and high GWP.

Grid-aware operation

Scheduling or shifting workloads, or running on UPS batteries, to help balance the grid.

Higher-voltage racks

As racks approach 1 MW, distribution voltage rises, with side cabinets for power and cooling.

Water-free cooling

Dry coolers and water-limited plants become standard, especially in water-scarce regions.

Next steps the paper recommends

Power

  • Engage design partners early on electricity supply.
  • Run a feasibility study with EPMS and DCIM.
  • Do capacity and arc flash studies with design software.
  • Digitalize single-line diagrams; use digital twins.

Cooling

  • Assess proposed liquid-cooled loads against existing site conditions.
  • Get expert review before retrofitting.
  • Use partners' thermal and fluid modeling tools.

Racks

  • Specify wider, deeper, taller, stronger racks.
  • Check slab ratings above 3,000 kg.
  • Plan cable trays for new layouts.
  • Validate independently and order early.

Software & services

  • Use DCIM, EPMS, BMS and reference architectures.
  • Build a digital twin to find constrained capacity.
  • Outsource if bandwidth or expertise is short.
