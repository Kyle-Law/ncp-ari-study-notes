An explainer of Best Practices Guide for Energy-Efficient Data Center Design, U.S. DOE Federal Energy Management Program and NREL, revised July 2024

Every watt that doesn’t reach a server is overhead.

Data centers can use many times the electricity of an office. The guide walks through IT equipment, room conditions, airflow, cooling, power, and heat reuse, and shows how to measure the result. Its core yardstick is PUE: total facility energy divided by the energy the IT equipment actually uses.

The guide puts an average data center at about 1.6; the most efficient facilities get below 1.1. A PUE of 1.0 would mean zero overhead.

1Four steps, in order of importance

Design is often rushed, and reliability and power density rightly come first. That leads to scaled-up office designs or “good enough” reuse of old specs. FEMP and NREL propose a priority order for sustainability, each step paired with a metric you can track.

  1. 1

    Reduce energy use

    Run IT intake air as warm as the thermal guidelines allow, use free cooling instead of compressors, and tune fans, pumps and UPS units.

    PUE
  2. 2

    Reuse the heat

    Let equipment discharge hotter air or water so the heat is useful elsewhere.

    ERE
  3. 3

    Reject the rest dry

    Send leftover heat to dry coolers instead of evaporating water in towers.

    WUE
  4. 4

    Go renewable

    Aim for 100% renewable energy, 100% of the time, on-site or from the grid region.

    CUE

A thread running through all four: higher temperatures coming out of the IT equipment make heat reuse and dry heat rejection easier.

2Start with the IT equipment

IT gear is the guide’s starting point because every watt saved there also removes the cooling and power-conversion overhead that would have served it. Even in a well-cooled facility, IT equipment can be more than half the total energy.

Cloud and colocation can lower first cost and may lower operating cost, especially compared with small on-premises rooms that lack round-the-clock staff, redundancy, and efficient design. The guide leaves the cloud-versus-on-premises choice to each organization.

Servers: idle capacity is the biggest waste

Enterprise servers typically run at 20–40% utilization. Raising utilization from low levels improves work-per-watt by about 50%. ENERGY STAR servers average about 30% more efficient than standard ones and are required in federal data centers. Virtualization is the main lever: fewer, busier machines.

Storage and network

Storage power rises roughly linearly with the number of drives, so right-size redundancy, move rarely used data offline (NAS or SAN), and use thin provisioning, which draws from a shared pool as needed instead of reserving fixed space per application. For networks, newer gear moves more data per watt, and Ethernet links can be stepped down to match the traffic actually flowing.

Power supplies: pick for where they’ll actually run

At $0.12/kWh, going from 75% to 85% power-supply efficiency saves roughly $2,000–$6,000 per rack per year for 10–25 kW racks, counting the knock-on reduction in UPS and cooling load.

Grouping equipment with similar heat density and temperature needs also helps, because each zone’s cooling can then run at its least energy-intensive set point. The same logic applies at building scale: consolidating scattered server rooms into one place makes efficiency measures easier to apply.

3How warm can the room be?

Cooling design starts from the inlet air conditions IT equipment is rated for. ASHRAE, working with manufacturers, widened its envelope in 2008; the 2021 edition defines a recommended range for efficient, reliable operation and wider allowable ranges (classes A1–A4) that manufacturers test for function, not long-term reliability. Design to the recommended range.

The catch: server fans speed up

Server fans follow internal temperature, so very warm inlet air makes them spin faster. Fan power rises with the cube of speed. The guide’s example: inlet air going from 77°F to 91°F can push fan speed up 30%, which more than doubles fan power. Even so, facility cooling savings usually outweigh it, as long as you stay near the recommended range.

ASHRAE has also added Class H1 for high-density servers with powerful CPUs, GPUs and memory. For those, the guide points toward direct liquid cooling (section 5).

4Air management: stop hot and cold air mixing

Good air management means cool supply air goes only into rack intakes and hot exhaust goes straight back to the cooling system. Two failure modes waste energy: bypass (cold air that skips the racks) and recirculation (hot exhaust drawn back into intakes, creating hot spots). Fix them and you can raise supply temperature, slow fans, and pack more power per square foot.

Practical rules from the guide

  • Keep at least 24 inches of clear height under a raised floor, and run an ongoing program to remove abandoned cables that block airflow.
  • Seal cable openings in floors and ceilings; use strip curtains or panels between rack tops and the return to stop “short-circuiting.”
  • Target the recommended range (about 65–80°F) at IT intakes. Buy equipment rated A2 or wider.
  • Raise supply air in small steps until the hottest intake sits near 80°F; 77–79°F is a practical set point. Do this only after fixing air management.
  • Control on IT intake temperatures (ideally read from the servers’ own sensors), not on return air temperature.
  • With overhead supply, use diffusers that drop air straight down into the cold aisle, not office diffusers that throw air sideways.
  • Watch under-floor air velocity near downflow units: it can create negative pressure that starves nearby racks.

Separated hot air often returns at 85°F or more (a server can raise air temperature by 10°F to over 40°F). That hotter return is what unlocks more economizer hours, lets standard package units use more of their capacity for sensible cooling, and allows warmer chilled water.

5Cooling systems

Size for today and tomorrow, and pay special attention to part-load performance; data centers rarely run at design peak.

Direct expansion (DX) units

Packaged DX units (CRACs and rooftop units) are common in smaller rooms. Improve them with multiple or variable-speed compressors, evaporative spray on air-cooled condensers, variable-speed condenser fans, air-side economizers, and, for water-cooled CRACs, a pre-cooling coil fed by condenser water when it’s cold enough. In dry climates, water-cooled CRACs with cooling towers can beat air-cooled units.

Central air handlers beat scattered units

Central systems use bigger, more efficient fans and motors, run well at part load with variable-speed drives, are easier to pair with economizers and containment, and avoid modular units “fighting” (one humidifying while its neighbor dehumidifies). Use oversized, short, straight ducts or a generous under-floor plenum: low pressure drop means low fan energy, around the clock.

Chilled water plants

For large facilities, a variable-speed water-cooled chiller with condenser water reset is usually the most efficient option. Run chilled water at 55°F or higher: chillers work more efficiently, coils stop accidentally dehumidifying, and free-cooling hours go up. Oversize cooling towers (smaller approach, slower fans), aim for a 5–7°F tower approach, and select pumps for efficiency at typical, not peak, flow.

Pumping upgrades include converting to primary-only variable flow, swapping 3-way valves for 2-way, removing balancing valves in favor of VFDs, and resetting differential pressure set points.

Free cooling with a water-side economizer

Air-side economizers bring in outside air whenever it is cooler than the return air (not the set point), which is why hot-aisle separation pays off. MERV 13 filters keep pollutant effects small; use a dew-point lockout to avoid humidity swings, and place intakes on the north side in the northern hemisphere. They are hard to retrofit, which is where water-side economizers come in. Many cool-climate data centers use only economizer cooling.

Thermal storage (for example, chilled water tanks charged by cooling towers on cool nights) cuts peak demand, improves reliability, and doubles as backup make-up water.

Direct liquid cooling

Water moves heat far better than air. Capturing heat at or near the chip allows warmer coolant, smaller fans, more economizer hours and, at high enough temperatures, dry coolers that use no water. Rising CPU/GPU/TPU power is pushing liquid cooling into the mainstream; high-performance computing racks went from about 60 kW in 2013 to beyond 125 kW. Most systems use a coolant distribution unit (CDU) between the facility loop and the IT loop.

Humidity: usually overdone

Most data centers don’t need humidification to stay above the recommended minimum (15.8°F dew point, about 10% RH). Tight humidity control is a legacy habit. When you do humidify, adiabatic methods also cool the air instead of heating it.

Controls

Put variable-speed drives on CRAH/CRAC fans and control raised-floor fans to under-floor pressure; better still, use variable air volume delivery. Have all units share sensors and set points, recalibrate humidity sensors, keep cooling coils above the dew point (chilled water above about 50°F), and reset chilled water warmer at low load.

6Electrical systems

Every device between the utility and the server sheds some power as heat, and that heat then has to be cooled. First question: does every load actually need UPS protection? A scientific computing facility may need far less than a bank.

UPS efficiency has a big multiplier

Fewer conversions, higher voltage

  • Keep voltage high for as long as possible to cut current, and keep conductors short and thick.
  • Place voltage regulators close to the load; use switch-mode power conditioning.
  • In PDUs with step-down transformers, a 176°F-rise dry-type transformer uses 13–21% less energy than a 302°F-rise unit, and transformers tend to be most efficient at 20–50% load. Use fewer transformer PDUs where you can.
  • Join utility demand-response programs: dim some lighting, shut idle equipment, or shift to backup generation at peak times.
  • Use LED lighting placed over aisles (not racks) with zoned occupancy sensors, especially in rarely visited UPS, battery and switchgear rooms.

7Use the waste heat

The hotter the air or water leaving the servers, the more useful it is. The best returns come from direct low-temperature uses such as preheating ventilation air or heating water; heat recovery chillers can also serve office heating. Reuse can cut or eliminate chiller use and water-evaporating towers, and it offsets fossil heating fuel.

What makes a heat reuse project work

  • A heat host close by, ideally adjacent, or a connection to a district loop.
  • A temperature the host can use directly, ideally without heat pumps.
  • Ideally, the same owner for the data center and the heat host.
  • A champion, plus incentives and policies that support reuse.

8Benchmarking: measure to manage

The guide recommends the PUE family of metrics plus a work-per-watt utilization metric suited to your workload (such as Gflops or transactions per watt). All are annual figures. Note that PUE judges the infrastructure only; it says nothing about how efficient the IT itself is.

Rack Cooling Index: the safety check on warmer rooms

The international versions of these KPIs live in ISO/IEC 30134 (parts covering PUE, renewable energy factor, server efficiency and utilization, energy reuse factor, cooling efficiency ratio, CUE and WUE).

Metering that’s worth having

Set targets first, then decide what to measure. Specify sensor accuracy that fits the range (±1.5°F sensors can’t resolve a 5°F chilled-water ΔT), use electromagnetic or ultrasonic flow meters and true-RMS three-phase power meters, trend everything for at least a year, use an open-protocol control system, and measure temperature and humidity at IT air inlets at ASHRAE-prescribed heights.

9If you remember eight things

  • Fix IT first: efficient servers and power supplies, higher utilization, virtualization. Savings cascade into cooling and power.
  • Design to ASHRAE’s recommended range and push intake air toward 80°F, gradually.
  • Separate hot and cold air with aisles, blanking panels, sealing and containment.
  • Prefer central, low-pressure-drop air systems with variable-speed fans, and chilled water at 55°F or above.
  • Use free cooling (air- or water-side) wherever the climate allows; consider liquid cooling for dense racks.
  • Right-size UPS and transformers so they run in their efficient load range; use eco modes where acceptable.
  • Run equipment hot enough at the outlet to reuse heat and reject the rest without water.
  • Meter properly and track PUE, ERE, WUE, CUE and RCI year over year.
