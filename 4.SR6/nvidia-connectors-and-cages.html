Connectors and cages

Everything on the page builds from that one family name. "SFP" is the base; the letter in front says how many electrical channels (lanes) the connector carries. The number after (28, 56, 112) hints at the per-lane signalling speed.

What "lanes" look like at the connector face

Each lane is an independent high-speed electrical channel. More lanes in the same kind of shell means more bandwidth per port. This is why an OSFP or QSFP-DD can carry twice what a QSFP carries at the same per-lane speed.

Schematic, not to scale. Lane counts are what matters; real connectors carry these lanes as gold pads on a small circuit board ("paddle card").

Plug and cage: the two halves

A plug is the end of a copper cable, active optical cable, or transceiver. A cage is the metal receptacle on the equipment's faceplate with an electrical connector at the back. The plug's paddle card slides into that connector. Whether something "fits" depends on the cage's shape, its connector, and how it removes heat.

The three OSFP versions

NVIDIA's 100G-PAM4 line (NDR InfiniBand, 400/800G Ethernet) is built on three connector and cage types: twin-port OSFP, single-port OSFP, and QSFP-DD. OSFP itself comes in three physical variants, and the difference that matters is where the heat sink lives.

"Twin-port" means one plug carries two independent 400G links (two optical connectors on the end). Newer NVIDIA docs call the finned version IHS and the flat version RHS.

Why the fins matter

Switch cages are open on top, so the plug has to bring its own cooling — hence the finned top. Adapter cards and some systems put a heat sink on the cage itself that "rides" on the plug, so the plug must be flat to make contact.

Cables use the same ends

The finned 800G twin-port OSFP end is also used to build passive copper cables, active copper cables and active optical cables, with twin-port OSFP, OSFP, QSFP112 or QSFP56 on the other end.

How QSFP stays backwards compatible

The QSFP family evolved over several generations but kept the same outer shape. A QSFP-DD cage adds a second row of contacts behind the first. An older 4-lane plug only has pads for the first row, so it engages those 4 lanes and simply leaves the back row unused.

All 8 lanes connect: 8 × 50G-PAM4 = 400GbE.
  • ✓QSFP-DD switch cages accept QSFP112, QSFP56 and QSFP28 devices, running them with 50G-PAM4 or 25G-NRZ signalling.
  • ✓QSFP112 cages (adapters and DPUs) accept QSFP56, QSFP28 and QSFP+ cables and transceivers.
  • ✓QSFP56 cages (switches and adapters) accept QSFP28 and QSFP+ cables and transceivers.
  • ✕QSFP-DD is not used for InfiniBand, and not for 100G-PAM4 Ethernet. It is a switch-only cage — ConnectX-6/7/8 adapters and BlueField-2/3 DPUs are never built with QSFP-DD.

Adapters: what exists and what doesn't

  • ✓QSA+ and QSA28 let a single-lane SFP+ or SFP28 device sit in a QSFP cage, passing through one channel.
  • ✕There is no QSFP-to-OSFP adapter. QSFP112 devices cannot be put into twin-port OSFP cages, and twin-port or single-port OSFP cages are not compatible with the QSFP line at all.

Where you'll find each cage

ConnectX-7 ships in both OSFP and QSFP112 versions, so check which cage your card has before ordering cables.

The short version

  • 1The prefix tells you lane count: SFP 1, QSFP 4, QSFP-DD and OSFP 8.
  • 2The QSFP family is backwards compatible: newer QSFP cages accept older QSFP plugs, which use only the first 4 lanes.
  • 3OSFP and QSFP are separate worlds; no adapter bridges them.
  • 4Within OSFP, match the top: finned plugs for open switch cages, flat plugs for cages with their own heat sink; twin-port for twin-port cages, single-port for adapters.
  • 5QSFP-DD is Ethernet-switch only. InfiniBand and NVIDIA adapters don't use it.
